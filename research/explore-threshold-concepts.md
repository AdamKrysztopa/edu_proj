# Explore: Threshold Concepts

**Question:** Can threshold concepts be identified reliably, does calling a concept "threshold" predict anything beyond bottlenecks, misconceptions and prerequisite graphs, and has teaching designed around one improved learning, retention or transfer (the §5.11 questions)?

**Verdict: keep at P2; rating lowered from "Moderate / contested" to "Low / contested"** (by the user's decision, departing from the stop rule as for PCK). The construct is widely used as vocabulary, but its evidence base does not reach "moderate":
- **Definitions:** philosophical critiques argue the definitions make threshold concepts impossible to isolate empirically (Rowbottom 2007; O'Donnell 2010; Salwén 2021), and that being "threshold" is relative to the learner (Rowbottom 2007).
- **Identification:** there is no settled method. Participants disagree about which concepts are thresholds (Barradell 2013), results depend on who is asked and how (Quinlan et al. 2013), and no inter-rater or cross-institution agreement statistic was found. Delphi studies reach consensus by design, which is not the same as reliability.
- **Measurement:** whether "threshold crossing" can be measured at all is an open question in the field's own synthesis (Nicola-Richmond et al. 2018).
- **Intervention:** the few controlled studies are small, mostly outside university physics, measure immediate outcomes, and test teaching *a concept* against not teaching it. None tests whether the *threshold* label adds predictive or instructional value beyond what the map already gets from bottlenecks (§5.1), misconceptions (§5.5) and concept inventories (§5.6).

A rating change without quasi-experimental evidence departs from the `/branch` stop rule. The case is the same as for PCK: the rule would protect a rating that has no controlled support of its own. If the stop rule is applied literally, keep "Moderate / contested" and put the qualifiers in the cell. The recommendation is to lower it.

**§9 kill criterion:** not met. Phase 1 has not yet produced bottleneck data.

The critiques converge on one point: the defining properties are either relative to the learner or are only recognisable after the learner has crossed. That is the circularity §5.11 already names. No defence found gives an operational test that separates a threshold concept from any other hard, important concept.

No study found reports inter-rater agreement (e.g. kappa) between independent identifiers, or agreement across institutions, for a list of threshold concepts. Delphi studies report percentage agreement and between-round stability (e.g. Sher, Yasmeen & Bashir 2026, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-025-08516-6), but both are produced by iterative feedback toward consensus, which is a different property from reliability. The physics papers found (Harrison & Serbanescu 2017; Serbanescu 2017; Wilson et al. 2010, cited in both) re-describe difficulties that PER had already established through inventories and interviews (compare §5.5 and §5.6). Serbanescu concludes that physics threshold concepts are "too many to count", so in physics the label adds no new identification method.

Each controlled study compares teaching a hard concept, often with misconception-directed methods, against not teaching it or teaching it conventionally. None compares a concept labelled "threshold" with an equally hard concept that is not, and none tests whether crossing predicts later performance beyond prerequisite mastery. None measures transfer or retention beyond one delayed post-test in a study without a control group. So the evidence supports the methods already on the map (§5.5 conflict and refutation, §5.6 diagnosis), not the construct.

## Against

The case *for* keeping "Moderate":
- The framework is widely adopted: four international conferences and "hundreds of papers" by 2013 (Quinlan et al. 2013). Schema-theory methods "compliment and strengthen" parts of it (Walker 2013).
- Two small randomized or experimental studies report immediate gains when teaching targets the concept (Aptyka et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025).
- In a transactional curriculum dialogue it can help teachers decide what to prioritise (Barradell 2013; Knight et al. 2014).

None of these is evidence that the threshold property is real, identifiable or predictive. Adoption is not validation. The randomized results come from outside university physics, with outcomes measured within days (Aptyka: Grade 10, eight schools; Ma: one class of 30).

A targeted search for a study that validates the identification of threshold concepts (agreement statistics) or tests the label's predictive value found none.

Held against §11: the intervention studies measure immediate performance or concept use in explanations, not transfer. Delphi consensus and teacher dialogue are the kind of plausible reconstruction that §11 warns against when they are not checked against learner data.

## Open questions

These are only the ones that would change the verdict:

- Is there any study reporting inter-rater or cross-institution agreement on a list of threshold concepts, especially in physics or engineering? Good agreement would answer the identification critique and support restoring "Moderate".
- Is there a controlled study comparing instruction on a concept identified as threshold with instruction on a matched non-threshold concept, or showing that crossing predicts later integrated performance beyond prerequisites? A positive result would give the construct predictive value; without one it remains vocabulary.
- Do Aptyka et al. (2025) and Ma et al. (2025) replicate with delayed or transfer outcomes at university level?
