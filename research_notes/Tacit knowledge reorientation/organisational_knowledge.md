# Organisational knowledge: loss, concentration, reconstruction from artefacts, and the commercial/legal landscape

Scope: evidence for a system that (1) reconstructs a company's knowledge from artefacts (docs, SOPs, tickets, incidents, code, Git, ADRs, messages, training material), (2) flags contradictions, undocumented decisions, missing rationale, suspected tacit knowledge, doc-vs-practice divergence and knowledge concentrated in people, and (3) sends only the residual to expensive human elicitation.

Evidence tags: **ESTABLISHED** (replicated, or primary legal text), **PROMISING** (one or a few solid studies, or a vendor capability that is documented but not independently evaluated), **SPECULATIVE** (position paper, vendor claim, or my inference). Dates are as of 2026-09-28. Legal statements are for information only and are not legal advice.

Identifier check: DOIs marked "(OA-verified)" were matched exactly in OpenAlex in this session. DOIs marked "(CR-verified)" were matched by title and author on Crossref. The other URLs were fetched or returned by search in this session unless they are flagged otherwise.

## 1. Knowledge loss and retention: what is known, what it costs, and what works

### Takeaway
Knowledge loss from retirement and turnover is well documented as a *risk* in nuclear, aerospace, utilities and software. Almost every retention method rests on case studies, though. Only in software has anyone measured knowledge loss at scale, and only there has the loss been linked to outcomes. I found no rigorous, generalisable dollar figure for the cost of knowledge loss.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED (as a framework).** DeLong, *Lost Knowledge: Confronting the Threat of an Aging Workforce* (Oxford UP, 2004; ISBN 9780195170979) is the standard practitioner reference. It sorts at-risk knowledge into human, social, cultural and structured forms and argues for HR-integrated retention, meaning both tacit and explicit knowledge are captured and transferred before departure. It is solution-oriented and based on case studies, not effect sizes — [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/lost-knowledge-9780195170979); [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/11422)
- **ESTABLISHED (as a typology).** de Holan & Phillips (2004), "Remembrance of Things Past? The Dynamics of Organizational Forgetting", *Management Science* 50(11). This is the canonical treatment of organisational forgetting, accidental or intentional, of knowledge that is new or established. Its key idea for this project is that new knowledge that was never embedded dissipates, while embedded knowledge degrades. DOI [10.1287/mnsc.1040.0273](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1040.0273) (OA-verified; 538 citations). The typology's labels are recalled from the paper and were not re-read in this session.
- **ESTABLISHED.** Walsh & Ungson (1991), "Organizational Memory", *Academy of Management Review*. This is the classic "retention bins" model: memory sits in individuals, culture, transformations (procedures), structures, ecology and external archives. Only some of those bins are artefacts that can be mined — DOI [10.2307/258607](https://doi.org/10.2307/258607) (CR-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED (nuclear practice).** The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) nuclear programme saw about 35% of its workforce retire over six years, with another 35% eligible within five. It built a knowledge-retention process in three steps: assess and prioritise the risk of losing critical knowledge, plan capture or adaptation, and monitor. Each employee gets a **Total Risk Factor** that combines an **Attrition Risk Factor** (projected departure date) and a **Position Risk Factor** (how unique or critical the knowledge is and how hard the position is to refill) — [OSTI/ETDEWEB record, "Approach of TVA nuclear to retaining critical knowledge in an ageing workforce"](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20842949) (summary from search; the full-text fetch failed); [IAEA publication "Knowledge Loss Risk Management in Nuclear Organizations"](https://www.iaea.org/publications/10921/knowledge-loss-risk-management-in-nuclear-organizations)
- **ESTABLISHED (guidance, not evidence of efficacy).** The IAEA publishes a series of knowledge-management guides. They include IAEA-TECDOC-1399 (*The nuclear power industry's ageing workforce: transfer of knowledge to the next generation*), TECDOC-1510 (*KM for nuclear industry operating organizations*), TECDOC-1586 (*Planning and execution of KM assist missions*) and an IAEA book on *Risk management of knowledge loss in nuclear industry organizations*. They share a stated rationale: retirees carry tacit knowledge that was "never before extracted" and that is critical to safe operation — [TECDOC-1399](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1399_web.pdf); [TECDOC-1510](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1510_web.pdf); [TECDOC-1586](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/TE_1586_web.pdf); [OSTI record, risk management of knowledge loss](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20837917)
- **PROMISING.** Massingham (2018), "Measuring the impact of knowledge loss: a longitudinal study", *Journal of Knowledge Management*. This is a single case: an Australian government department, 2008–2013, 118 of 150 invited (79%) engineering and technical staff, surveyed annually. It developed five constructs for knowledge loss: knowledge resources, psychological contract, learning-organisation capacity, risk management and organisational problems. Its conclusion is that losses show up as reduced capability in strategic activities at the organisational level and as inefficiencies at the individual level — DOI [10.1108/jkm-08-2016-0338](https://doi.org/10.1108/jkm-08-2016-0338) (CR-verified); [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/jkm-08-2016-0338/full/html)
- **ESTABLISHED (classic quantitative evidence of depreciation).** Argote, Beckman & Epple (1990), "The persistence and transfer of learning in industrial settings", *Management Science*, found that production knowledge in Liberty-ship yards depreciated rapidly. OpenAlex only produced an uncertain match: a reprint chapter, [10.1016/b978-0-7506-9850-4.50014-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/b978-0-7506-9850-4.50014-2). The original journal DOI, 10.1287/mnsc.36.2.140, comes from memory and was **not verified** in this session.
- **SPECULATIVE (anecdotal, but widely cited).** In NASA's "Saturn V" knowledge-loss story, blueprints for the F-1 and Saturn V survive. What was lost is the institutional knowledge of how the as-built vehicle differed from the drawings, which stayed with the engineers when Apollo ended — [The Space Review, F-1 after Apollo](https://www.thespacereview.com/article/3724/1); [NASA APPEL podcast ep. 114, "Last stop before retirement"](https://appel.nasa.gov/podcast/episode-114-last-stop-before-retirement/). These are secondary and popular sources; I found no peer-reviewed NASA quantification.
- **SPECULATIVE (vendor/press claims; do not reuse without a primary source).** Two widely repeated figures should be treated as unsourced. One is that "formal documentation holds about 20% of what employees know" (attributed to "MIT research"); the other is Interloom's CEO saying "about 70% of operational decisions have never been formally documented". Both appear only in press or vendor material — [Fortune via Yahoo, 23 Mar 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/exclusive-interloom-startup-capturing-tacit-070000121.html); search summary of vendor blogs.

### Inferences
- The retention literature's evidence is mostly case studies and practitioner frameworks. Claims that mentoring, communities of practice, exit interviews or storytelling "work" are PROMISING at best. Reliable outcome data exists only where the loss can be measured from logs, as in software (section 3).
- The TVA/IAEA two-factor score (attrition × criticality) is a person-level prior. A system that reconstructs from artefacts can refine the **criticality** term: the Position Risk Factor stops being a manager's judgement and becomes "how much of what this person touches is *not* covered by artefacts".

### Gaps
- I found no credible, peer-reviewed dollar cost of knowledge loss. Figures that circulate, such as vendor surveys of "hours lost searching", were not traced to primary data and are omitted.
- I could not confirm the year or series number of the IAEA "Knowledge Loss Risk Management in Nuclear Organizations" publication; the page did not expose them in the fetch. Nor could I read TVA's exact scoring scales, because the IAEA NKM wiki's DNS failed.
- "Great crew change" (oil and gas): I found no primary quantitative source in this pass. It is mostly industry-press framing.

## 2. Organisational learning and knowledge-transfer theory: what matters for design

### Takeaway
Theory agrees on three points that matter for this design. First, transfer is hampered mainly by recipient capacity and causal ambiguity, not by unwillingness. Second, groups know "who knows what" (transactive memory), and that directory is itself an asset that can be lost. Third, codification and personalisation are complementary strategies. SECI's tacit→explicit "conversion" is contested, so the tool should not promise to convert tacit knowledge; it should locate and scope it.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED.** Szulanski (1996), "Exploring internal stickiness: impediments to the transfer of best practice within the firm", *Strategic Management Journal*, covers 122 transfers of 38 practices in 8 firms. The largest barriers were the recipient's lack of absorptive capacity, **causal ambiguity** and an arduous source–recipient relationship; motivation mattered less — DOI [10.1002/smj.4250171105](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.4250171105) (OA-verified; about 7,900 citations). The sample figures are recalled from the paper.
- **ESTABLISHED.** Argote & Ingram (2000), "Knowledge Transfer: A Basis for Competitive Advantage in Firms", *OBHDP*. Knowledge sits in reservoirs (members, tools, tasks and their networks), and transfer is easier when the reservoirs move together, for example when people move with their tools — DOI [10.1006/obhd.2000.2893](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.2000.2893) (OA-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Wegner (1987) introduced transactive memory, the group's shared directory of who knows what — DOI [10.1007/978-1-4612-4634-3_9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-4634-3_9) (CR-verified). Lewis (2003) built the field scale for it, with three dimensions: specialisation, credibility and coordination — DOI [10.1037/0021-9010.88.4.587](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.88.4.587) (OA-verified; about 1,100 citations)
- **ESTABLISHED (widely cited) / contested.** Nonaka (1994), "A Dynamic Theory of Organizational Knowledge Creation", *Organization Science*, is the SECI source: socialisation, externalisation, combination, internalisation — DOI [10.1287/orsc.5.1.14](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.5.1.14) (OA-verified; about 17,800 citations). The book is Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995) — DOI [10.1093/oso/9780195092691.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195092691.001.0001) (CR-verified). The critique is Gourlay (2006), "Conceptualizing Knowledge Creation: A Critique of Nonaka's Theory", *Journal of Management Studies*: the empirical base is weak and anecdotal, and the tacit/explicit "conversion" is conceptually problematic — DOI [10.1111/j.1467-6486.2006.00637.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2006.00637.x) (OA-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED (framework).** Hansen, Nohria & Tierney (1999), "What's Your Strategy for Managing Knowledge?", *HBR*, contrasts codification (people-to-documents reuse, e.g. Ernst & Young, Andersen) with personalisation (people-to-people, e.g. McKinsey, Bain). It recommends an 80/20 mix with one strategy dominant — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/whats-your-strategy-for-managing-knowledge). A refinement argues the right mix can sit anywhere from 80/20 to 20/80 and differs by unit: Scheepers, Venkitachalam & Gibbs (2004), *JSIS* — DOI [10.1016/j.jsis.2004.08.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsis.2004.08.003) (CR-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Communities of practice: Wenger (1998), *Communities of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity* — DOI [10.1017/cbo9780511803932](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511803932) (CR-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED (IR subfield).** Expertise retrieval, i.e. finding experts from documents, is reviewed in Balog, Fang, de Rijke, Serdyukov & Si (2012), *Foundations and Trends in IR* — DOI [10.1561/1500000024](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000024) (CR-verified). The earlier systems are McDonald & Ackerman (2000), "Expertise Recommender", CSCW — DOI [10.1145/358916.358994](https://doi.org/10.1145/358916.358994) (CR-verified) — and Mockus & Herbsleb (2002), "Expertise Browser", ICSE, which identifies expertise from version-control changes — DOI [10.1145/581400.581401](https://doi.org/10.1145/581400.581401) (CR-verified)

### Inferences
- Szulanski's "causal ambiguity" is the organisational name for **missing rationale**. An artefact analyser that flags *what* is done without *why* is targeting the barrier with the most empirical support.
- Transactive memory implies that the loss when a senior engineer leaves includes the **pointer structure** ("ask Ana about the flare system") as well as their content knowledge. Expert-finding from artefacts (Balog; Mockus & Herbsleb) reconstructs part of that directory. Doing so is also exactly the processing that employment law restricts (section 6).
- In Hansen's terms, the proposed system is a codification engine that decides where personalisation (elicitation) is still needed. That is a principled way to set the 80/20 split per domain, rather than by fiat. SPECULATIVE.

### Gaps
- There is no meta-analysis of TMS or knowledge-transfer interventions in this pass. The citation counts show influence, not intervention efficacy.

## 3. Software engineering: public, zero-participant evidence on concentration, loss, documentation and rationale

### Takeaway
Software is the only domain with large-scale, reproducible measures of knowledge concentration and loss. Truck factor, knowledge-at-risk, abandoned files and ownership are all computed from Git with no participants. Documentation defects and missing rationale are catalogued at scale, and 2024–2026 LLM studies show that rationale recovery has **high recall and low precision**. One recent argument is that AI-generated code breaks the authorship-equals-knowledge proxy.

### Cited Findings: concentration and turnover
- **ESTABLISHED.** Avelino, Passos, Hora & Valente (2016), "A Novel Approach for Estimating Truck Factors", ICPC. They studied 133 popular GitHub projects, and **65% have a truck factor ≤ 2**. In a survey covering 67 systems, 84% of valid answers agreed or partially agreed that the identified authors are the main authors, but only 53% were positive or partially positive about the estimated truck factor itself — DOI [10.1109/icpc.2016.7503718](https://doi.org/10.1109/icpc.2016.7503718) (OA-verified); [arXiv 1604.06766](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.06766)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Algorithms disagree. Ferreira, Valente & Ferreira (2017), "A Comparison of Three Algorithms for Computing Truck Factors", ICPC — DOI [10.1109/icpc.2017.35](https://doi.org/10.1109/icpc.2017.35) (OA-verified). The extended version is Ferreira, Mombach, Valente & Ferreira (2019), *Software Quality Journal* — DOI [10.1007/s11219-019-09457-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11219-019-09457-2) (CR-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Rigby, Zhu, Donadelli & Mockus (2016), "Quantifying and mitigating turnover-induced knowledge loss: case studies of Chrome and a project at Avaya", ICSE, applies financial risk methods to abandoned files, defining **Knowledge-at-Risk (KaR)**, by analogy to Value-at-Risk, and Expected Shortfall. Its findings:
  1. Projects face losses more than 3× the expected loss.
  2. Historical simulation shows losses more than 5× expected.
  3. Simplistic truck-factor estimates exaggerate the potential for loss.
  4. Successor recommendation is correct 34–48% of the time, and having successors cuts expected loss by up to 15%.

  DOI [10.1145/2884781.2884851](https://doi.org/10.1145/2884781.2884851) (DOI printed in the paper's PDF); [PDF](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~pcr/paper/Rigby2016ICSE.pdf)
- **ESTABLISHED (replicated).** Nassif & Robillard (2017), "Revisiting Turnover-Induced Knowledge Loss in Software Projects", ICSME, replicated Rigby et al. on Chromium plus 7 other open-source projects. All the projects had similar loss distributions, but **extreme loss can be more severe** than Rigby et al. found, and abandoned files often stay in the system for long periods — DOI [10.1109/icsme.2017.64](https://doi.org/10.1109/icsme.2017.64) (OA-verified); [PDF](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/icsme2017.pdf)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Avelino, Constantinou, Valente & Serebrenik (2019), "On the abandonment and survival of open source projects". Of 1,932 popular GitHub projects, **315 (16%) were abandoned** because their truck-factor developers left, and 128 of those (41%) survived when new core developers took over. The main barriers for the new maintainers were lack of time and difficulty getting push access — [arXiv 1906.08058](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08058)
- **PROMISING.** Robillard (2021), "Turnover-Induced Knowledge Loss in Practice", ESEC/FSE, interviewed 27 developers and managers at 3 companies. It characterises 41 knowledge-loss situations and draws 20 observations. Two of them: internal transfers can amount to complete departure as far as knowledge is concerned, and leavers often do not fully disengage, so they remain reachable — DOI [10.1145/3468264.3473923](https://doi.org/10.1145/3468264.3473923) (CR-verified)
- **PROMISING.** Jabrayilzade, Evtikhiev, Tüzün & Kovalenko (2022), "Bus Factor in Practice", ICSE-SEIP. A survey of 269 engineers found the bus factor seen as an important problem. The authors propose a **multimodal** bus factor that adds code-review and meeting data to version control, tested on 13 JetBrains projects — DOI [10.1109/icse-seip55303.2022.9793985](https://doi.org/10.1109/icse-seip55303.2022.9793985) (CR-verified); [arXiv 2202.01523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01523)
- **PROMISING (tooling).** Cury & Avelino (2024), "Knowledge Islands", is an open-source tool that computes truck factor per folder and per file and visualises where knowledge is concentrated — [arXiv 2408.08733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08733)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Bird, Nagappan, Murphy, Gall & Devanbu (2011), "Don't Touch My Code! Examining the effects of ownership on software quality", ESEC/FSE, studied Windows Vista and Windows 7. The number of low-expertise (minor) contributors and the top owner's share of ownership are related to both pre-release faults and post-release failures — DOI [10.1145/2025113.2025119](https://doi.org/10.1145/2025113.2025119); [PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/bird2011dtm.pdf)
- **ESTABLISHED (related).** Mockus (2010), "Organizational volatility and its effects on software defects", FSE — DOI [10.1145/1882291.1882311](https://doi.org/10.1145/1882291.1882311) (OA-verified). I did not re-read its findings in this session.
- **SPECULATIVE (single-author position paper).** Wheeler (2026), "The Substrate Collapse: AI Code Generation Invalidates Authorship-Based Knowledge Metrics", argues that truck factor, degree-of-authorship and degree-of-knowledge all assume that writing code implies understanding it. When agents generate code and humans merge it, that assumption fails, so the metrics are "invalidated as a class" — [arXiv 2606.20882](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20882)
- **SPECULATIVE.** Chua & Ajila (2026), "Knowledge Lever Risk Management for SE", is a four-phase framework (Audit, Alignment, Activation, Assurance) validated with Monte Carlo simulation, and it treats ADRs and pair programming as levers — [arXiv 2604.23257](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23257)

### Cited Findings: onboarding and documentation
- **ESTABLISHED.** Steinmacher, Silva, Gerosa & Redmiles, a systematic literature review of barriers faced by newcomers to open-source projects (*Information and Software Technology*, 2015). Documentation problems are one of the barrier categories — DOI [10.1016/j.infsof.2014.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infsof.2014.11.001) (OA-verified). The follow-up guidelines are "Let Me In", *IEEE Software* 2019 — DOI [10.1109/ms.2018.110162131](https://doi.org/10.1109/ms.2018.110162131) (CR-verified)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Aghajani et al. (2019), "Software Documentation Issues Unveiled", ICSE, analysed **878 artefacts** from mailing lists, Stack Overflow, issue trackers and pull requests and built a **taxonomy of 162 documentation issue types**. The dominant issues are outdated, incomplete and inconsistent information — DOI [10.1109/icse.2019.00122](https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2019.00122) (OA-verified); [ICSE page](https://2019.icse-conferences.org/details/icse-2019-Technical-Papers/49/Software-Documentation-Issues-Unveiled)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Aghajani et al. (2020), "Software Documentation: The Practitioners' Perspective", ICSE, ran two surveys of 146 practitioners on which issues matter and which documentation types matter for which tasks — DOI [10.1145/3377811.3380405](https://doi.org/10.1145/3377811.3380405) (CR-verified; OpenAlex's title match failed because the Crossref title is truncated to "Software documentation")

### Cited Findings: design rationale and decision recovery
- **PROMISING.** Rationale can be extracted from existing artefacts. Rogers et al. (2012) extracted rationale from Chrome bug reports, ICSE — DOI [10.1109/icse.2012.6227091](https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2012.6227091) (CR-verified). Bhat, Shumaiev, Biesdorf et al. (2017) used machine learning to extract design decisions from issue-management systems, ECSA/LNCS — DOI [10.1007/978-3-319-65831-5_10](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-65831-5_10) (CR-verified). Dhaouadi, Oakes & Famelis (2024) built a rationale dataset from Linux kernel OOM-killer commit messages, ICPC — DOI [10.1145/3643916.3644413](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643916.3644413) (OA-verified)
- **PROMISING.** Dhar, Vaidhyanathan & Varma (2024), "Can LLMs Generate Architectural Design Decisions?", found that GPT-4 zero-shot produces relevant, accurate decisions from an ADR's Context, but below human level — [arXiv 2403.01709](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.01709)
- **PROMISING, with a key number.** Zhou, Li & Liang (2025), "Using LLMs in Generating Design Rationale for Software Architecture Decisions", used 100 problems drawn from Stack Overflow, GitHub issues and GitHub discussions, with experts' rationale as ground truth. Across 5 LLMs and 3 prompting strategies, **precision was 0.267–0.278, recall 0.627–0.715 and F1 0.351–0.389**, and **64–69% of the LLM's rationale arguments were not mentioned by the human experts** — [arXiv 2504.20781](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20781)
- **PROMISING (preliminary).** Karan, Dhar & Soliman (2026) had 4 LLMs extract architectural design decisions from commits, against 30 developer-written ADDs. BERTScore-F1 was above 0.81, but the outputs are "too long, implementation-focused, and **miss the rationale**" — [arXiv 2609.03721](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03721)
- **PROMISING (feasibility).** GADR (2026) is a multi-agent workflow that builds Nygard-format ADRs from raw meeting transcripts. It captured most expert-identified decisions, but RAG enrichment "risk[s] transcript-unfaithful content" — [arXiv 2608.17694](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17694)
- **PROMISING.** Repository-level documentation generation: RepoAgent (Luo et al., EMNLP 2024 demo) — DOI [10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-demo.46](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-demo.46) (OA-verified)

### Inferences
- The zero-participant measures (truck factor, KaR/Expected Shortfall, abandoned files) are all **authorship-based**. They estimate who *touched* what, not what is undocumented. A residual computation needs a second axis, *artefact coverage*: does a document, ADR, test, or commit or issue rationale explain this component? The SE literature measures the two axes separately and never joins them (see Implications).
- LLM rationale recovery has high recall and low precision, and roughly two-thirds of its arguments are unsupported by experts. That profile suits **gap and hypothesis generation** ("probably decided because X — confirm?") and does not suit an authoritative reconstruction. This supports routing LLM-reconstructed rationale to humans for confirmation, which is the residual-elicitation design itself.
- Wheeler's argument, even if overstated, marks a real validity threat for 2026+ repositories. Authorship metrics should be weighted by review participation and discussion rather than raw commits, as Jabrayilzade's multimodal bus factor already suggests.

### Gaps
- "Jabrayilov et al. 2016", as named in the brief, did **not resolve** in OpenAlex or Crossref, and no match was found by search. It may be a garbled citation, perhaps mixing Jabrayilzade et al. 2022 with 2016 truck-factor work. Do not cite it until it is located.
- DeepWiki, RepoAgent and similar tools have no independent accuracy evaluation against held-out expert knowledge; I found none.

## 4. Industrial and IT operations: maintenance text, incidents, and "work-as-imagined vs work-as-done"

### Takeaway
Maintenance work orders and incident postmortems are rich, messy, public-ish sources of troubleshooting knowledge. Technical language processing (TLP) has benchmarks for them. Doc-vs-practice divergence has a mature theoretical frame in Hollnagel's work-as-imagined versus work-as-done, and a mature computational one in process-mining conformance checking. Nobody appears to have joined these to *document* corpora; conformance checking needs an event log and a formal model, not an SOP in prose.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED (agenda) / PROMISING (methods).** Brundage, Sexton, Hodkiewicz, Dima & Lukens (2021), "Technical language processing: Unlocking maintenance knowledge", *Manufacturing Letters*, from NIST and UWA. It argues that general NLP fails on terse, jargon-heavy technical text and calls for human-in-the-loop, domain-adapted TLP — DOI [10.1016/j.mfglet.2020.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mfglet.2020.11.001) (OA-verified; FWCI about 100). The related study of categorisation errors in maintenance work-order data entry is Sexton et al. (2019), PHM — DOI [10.36001/phmconf.2019.v11i1.790](https://doi.org/10.36001/phmconf.2019.v11i1.790) (CR-verified)
- **PROMISING (benchmark).** MaintIE (Bikaun et al., LREC-COLING 2024) is a fine-grained annotation schema and benchmark for information extraction from maintenance short texts — DOI [10.63317/3cuvz8qfmako](https://doi.org/10.63317/3cuvz8qfmako) (CR-verified; an unusual DOI prefix, so check that it resolves)
- **ESTABLISHED (datasets).** MaintNet is an open library of maintenance logbook text from aviation, automotive and facilities, with preprocessing tools — [arXiv 2005.12443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12443); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.coling-demos.2/). A public **excavator dataset of 5,485 work orders for 5 excavators** (UWA Prognostics Data Library) is used in TLP research — [NIST nestor datasets](https://pages.nist.gov/nestor/nestor/datasets/). The UWA host failed DNS lookup in this session.
- **PROMISING.** Ahmed et al. (2023), "Recommending Root-Cause and Mitigation Steps for Cloud Incidents using Large Language Models", ICSE, evaluated LLMs on Microsoft production incidents — DOI [10.1109/icse48619.2023.00149](https://doi.org/10.1109/icse48619.2023.00149) (CR-verified). The incident counts and scores were not re-read in this session.
- **ESTABLISHED (public corpora).** Public incident and postmortem collections include Dan Luu's curated list of public postmortems — [github.com/danluu/post-mortems](https://github.com/danluu/post-mortems) — and the VOID (Verica Open Incident Database), a public incident-report database — [thevoid.community](https://www.thevoid.community/). The page rendered empty in the fetch, so its current size was not verified.
- **ESTABLISHED (concept).** Hollnagel's **Work-as-Imagined** is an idealised view of the formal task environment that ignores how performance must adjust to changing conditions. **Work-as-Done** is what actually happens. Safety-II argues that safety management must be based on work-as-done — [EUROCONTROL, *From Safety-I to Safety-II: A White Paper* (2013)](https://skybrary.aero/sites/default/files/bookshelf/2437.pdf); [EUROCONTROL HindSight 25, "Work-as-imagined & work-as-done"](https://www.eurocontrol.int/sites/default/files/publication/files/hindsight25.pdf); book: Hollnagel, *Safety-I and Safety-II* — DOI [10.1201/9781315607511](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315607511) (CR-verified; 2018 print of the 2014 book)
- **ESTABLISHED (technique).** Process-mining **conformance checking** compares an event log (the as-is process) with a reference model (the to-be process) and reports skipped, extra and reordered steps — [processmining.org, conformance](https://www.processmining.org/conformance.html); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformance_checking). Mining constraints from reference models to detect best-practice violations in event logs is PROMISING — [arXiv 2407.02336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.02336)

### Inferences
- A **doc-vs-practice divergence** detector can use three signals, in decreasing order of rigour:
  1. Conformance checking, where an event log exists (ticket state transitions, CMMS work orders, CI/CD pipelines).
  2. Text-level contradiction between an SOP and executed records (work-order narratives, incident timelines).
  3. Frequency signals: steps that SOPs require but records never mention, or recurring workarounds in tickets that no SOP mentions.

  Signal (2) is the novel LLM part and has no benchmark I found. SPECULATIVE.
- Incident postmortems are the richest public source of work-as-done, and of "we discovered the runbook was wrong".

### Gaps
- I found no public dataset that pairs **SOPs with execution logs** for the same process, which is what a doc-vs-practice benchmark needs. Process-mining benchmark logs (e.g. BPI Challenge) come with no prose SOPs; this was not searched in depth.
- I found no studies quantifying how often SOPs are deviated from in a given industry.

## 6. Legal, privacy and security (information, not legal advice)

### Takeaway
In the EU, mining employees' messages and Git history to infer who knows what counts as processing employee personal data. In Germany it is a **co-determined technical monitoring device** whenever it is *objectively capable* of assessing behaviour or performance. Such a system can also slide into **AI Act high-risk** territory if it is used for task allocation, evaluating performance, or evaluating learning outcomes. The Annex III obligations now apply from **2 December 2027**. Artefact-level reconstruction without person attribution is far less exposed. Copyright: EU commercial text-and-data mining (TDM) is permitted under DSM Art. 4 unless the rightholder has opted out, but CC-NC material and paywalled standards need separate licences. Security: indirect prompt injection through ingested documents is a demonstrated attack class.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED (legal text).** **AI Act Annex III**:
  - Point 3 (education and vocational training) covers systems intended to: (a) determine access or admission; (b) **evaluate learning outcomes, including when used to steer the learning process**; (c) assess the appropriate level of education; (d) monitor prohibited behaviour during tests.
  - Point 4 (employment) covers (a) recruitment and selection, and (b) systems that make decisions affecting terms of work, promotion or termination, that **allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits**, or that **monitor and evaluate performance and behaviour**.

### Inferences
- **Design implication (SPECULATIVE, informational).** Separate two modes. *Artefact-coverage analysis* asks "this subsystem's rationale is undocumented". *Person-attributed concentration* asks "only Ana's commits and messages explain it". The second mode is where GDPR's legitimate-interest balancing, the German co-determination veto and potentially AI Act Annex III(4)(b) apply, if outputs feed task allocation or performance evaluation.

  Safer defaults:
  - aggregate or pseudonymised concentration scores;
  - expert *consent to be named*, as in skills profiles that users manage themselves (the People Skills pattern);
  - no use of the outputs for evaluation;
  - a works agreement (Betriebsvereinbarung) template.
- If the education side of the product *evaluates learners' learning outcomes*, Annex III(3)(b) applies to it from 2 December 2027. Cohort-level diagnosis that does not evaluate individuals is on the safer side of the Art. 6(3) derogation. This is not a determination.
- Commercial ingestion of public web content, textbooks and OER is workable in the EU under Art. 4 only with an opt-out-respecting crawler and licence filtering (exclude NC and ND). **ISO/IEC standards are paywalled and copyright-licensed**, so ingesting them needs a licence; the Art. 4 exception requires lawful access and does not override licence terms that restrict it. This is an inference and was not checked against ISO's licence terms.
- Every ingested company document is an injection vector. Needed controls:
  - retrieval that enforces source ACLs per user;
  - treating retrieved text as data, never instructions;
  - provenance on every reconstructed claim.

## 7. KM concepts that map onto a "human knowledge residual"

### Takeaway
Knowledge audits, knowledge mapping, gap analysis and knowledge-loss risk assessment (KLRA) are established *practices*. All of them use interviews and surveys to decide what is critical and who holds it. None computes coverage from artefacts. The closest quantitative precedents are TVA's two-factor person score and Rigby's Knowledge-at-Risk.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED (practice).** TVA/IAEA KLRA scores people on two factors: attrition (time to expected departure) and position criticality (uniqueness, criticality, difficulty of refilling), combined into a total risk factor that sets priorities for capture — [OSTI TVA record](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20842949); [IAEA KLRM publication](https://www.iaea.org/publications/10921/knowledge-loss-risk-management-in-nuclear-organizations); [CEWD/UIC Knowledge Loss Risk Assessment worksheet](https://www.cewd.org/documents/UIC-KnowledgeLossAssessment.pdf) (not fetched)
- **ESTABLISHED (method).** Rigby et al.'s KaR and Expected Shortfall give a **probabilistic** loss measure (quantiles of the loss distribution under turnover scenarios) rather than a point truck factor — DOI [10.1145/2884781.2884851](https://doi.org/10.1145/2884781.2884851)
- **PROMISING.** Massingham's five knowledge-loss constructs serve as a survey instrument for organisational impact — DOI [10.1108/jkm-08-2016-0338](https://doi.org/10.1108/jkm-08-2016-0338)
- **ESTABLISHED (SE KM review).** Rus & Lindvall (2002), "Knowledge management in software engineering", *IEEE Software* — DOI [10.1109/ms.2002.1003450](https://doi.org/10.1109/ms.2002.1003450) (CR-verified)

### Inferences
- The missing term in every existing method is **artefact coverage**. KLRA asks managers how unique someone's knowledge is. An artefact reconstruction can *measure* one part of it: how much of the knowledge attached to a component or task is recoverable without that person. See Implications (2).

### Gaps
- There are no peer-reviewed validation studies of KLRA scores against later realised loss. I found none; this is an opportunity for a retrospective study on OSS data.

## 8. Implications

### Takeaway
Public OSS ecosystems give a ready-made, zero-participant "organisation": code, Git history, issues, pull requests, reviews, mailing lists, ADRs, docs and real departures. On that data a residual computation can be validated retrospectively. Hold out a departed maintainer, then ask whether the model predicted the files and decisions that later caused trouble. The commercial gap is coverage accounting and elicitation targeting, not search.

### (1) Candidate zero-participant testbeds
- **OSS repositories with departures and ground truth** (ESTABLISHED as research substrates):
  - the 133-project truck-factor corpus — [Avelino et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.06766);
  - the 1,932 projects with 315 abandonments and 128 survivals — [Avelino et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08058);
  - Chromium plus 7 projects from the knowledge-loss replication — [Nassif & Robillard 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/icsme.2017.64).

  Ground truth comes from post-departure outcomes: abandoned files, defect-inducing changes to abandoned code, and "does anyone know why…" issues.
- **Rationale and decision ground truth:**
  - the Linux kernel OOM-killer rationale dataset — [Dhaouadi et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643916.3644413);
  - the 100 architecture-decision problems from Stack Overflow and GitHub with expert rationale — [Zhou, Li & Liang 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20781);
  - the 30 developer-written ADDs — [Karan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03721);
  - public ADR directories in OSS repositories (not enumerated here).
- **Documentation defects:** the 878-artefact, 162-type taxonomy can serve as a labelled scheme for contradiction and staleness detection — [Aghajani et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2019.00122)
- **Apache Software Foundation projects** combine public mailing lists, Jira, Git and votes, which suits decision mining; the issue-tracker decision-extraction precedent is [Bhat et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-65831-5_10). This is an inference: the corpus was not re-surveyed this session.
- **Operations:** public postmortems — [danluu/post-mortems](https://github.com/danluu/post-mortems), [VOID](https://www.thevoid.community/)
- **Maintenance:** [MaintNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12443), the excavator work orders (5,485 WOs) via [NIST nestor](https://pages.nist.gov/nestor/nestor/datasets/), and the [MaintIE](https://doi.org/10.63317/3cuvz8qfmako) benchmark
- **Auto-generated baselines to beat:** DeepWiki pages for the same repositories — [Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/deepwiki) — and [RepoAgent](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-demo.46). This allows a direct comparison: what does the generated wiki assert that expert-written docs or ADRs contradict?

### (2) What a knowledge-at-risk / residual computation could look like (SPECULATIVE design, grounded in the cited pieces)
For each knowledge unit *u* (a component, file cluster, process step, decision or SOP section):
1. **Coverage C(u) ∈ [0,1].** This is the share of *u*'s required explanatory elements (what, how, **why**, constraints, failure modes) that is supported by artefacts with provenance. Examples: an ADR, doc section, test, commit or PR rationale, incident note. Rationale should be weighted highest, since it is Szulanski's causal ambiguity and the most commonly missing element in the ADR/LLM studies. Because LLM rationale recovery has about 0.27 precision and about 0.7 recall, recovered "why" counts as a *hypothesis* (partial credit, flagged for confirmation), not as coverage.
2. **Consistency K(u).** This counts contradictions between sources about *u* (doc vs code, SOP vs tickets or work orders, ADR vs later commits) and staleness (doc last touched well before the code). The Aghajani taxonomy can serve as labels.
3. **Concentration H(u).** This is the distribution of evidence of understanding over people. Use authorship, **review participation, discussion authorship and meeting data** (the multimodal bus factor), and down-weight raw authorship of AI-generated code (Wheeler's objection). Summarise as an effective number of knowers, or a per-unit truck factor.
4. **Exposure.** Compute **KaR/Expected Shortfall** over turnover scenarios (Rigby), with departure probabilities from TVA-style attrition factors where HR data may lawfully be used, and uniform or historical rates otherwise. Weight by criticality (dependency centrality, incident history, safety relevance).
5. **Residual R(u)** = criticality × (1 − C) × f(K) × P(all knowers leave), where f(K) inflates the score when contradictions exist. Rank the units by R. The top of the ranking is the **elicitation agenda**, and each item carries a concrete probe, e.g. "Commit a1b2 changed the retry limit from 3 to 7; no issue, PR or ADR says why." That makes human elicitation targeted and short.
6. **Validation on OSS (zero participants).** Freeze a repository at time *t*, before a truck-factor developer leaves; compute R; then test whether high-R units disproportionately become abandoned files, attract more defects, or generate "why is this like this?" issues after *t*. This is a retrospective, falsifiable test with public data.
7. **Privacy mode.** Report H(u) as counts or pseudonyms by default, and name people only with their consent, per section 6.

### (3) Commercial white space (SPECULATIVE)
- **Coverage/residual accounting as the product's core output.** Existing tools retrieve (Glean, Copilot, Rovo), verify card by card (Guru), generate docs from code (DeepWiki, Swimm) or capture demonstrations (Dozuki, Augmentir, Tulip). None found reports what the artefacts *cannot* explain, per critical unit, with contradictions and named, consenting knowers.
- **Targeted elicitation.** Capture tools record what experts choose to show, while this system would generate the questions from the gaps. That is the bridge to the project's research on expert elicitation.
- **Cross-source contradiction and doc-vs-practice detection.** Process mining handles event logs; nothing found handles prose SOPs against tickets and work orders.
- **Compliance by design.** An EU-deployable variant ships with pseudonymised concentration, a works-agreement template, no evaluation use (staying outside Annex III(4)(b) and within Art. 6(3)), ACL-enforcing retrieval and injection-resistant ingestion. Incumbents' expert-inference features (Viva Topics, now People Skills) show that the demand is real and that governance is the friction.

### Gaps
- None of the residual formulas above has been tested. The OSS retrospective in step 6 is the cheapest falsification test available.
- There is no public corpus pairing SOPs with execution logs for doc-vs-practice validation.
