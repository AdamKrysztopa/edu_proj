# Synthetic / simulated learners and simulated experts as surrogates for human cohorts: a sceptical evidence note

Scope: what LLM-simulated students and experts can realistically model, where they fail, whether they reproduce real error distributions, how they must be grounded and validated, for a project that wants an "evidence-grounded surrogate cohort" before any real participants exist. Date of search: 2026-09-28.

Evidence tags: **ESTABLISHED** = replicated or peer-reviewed with consistent results across studies; **PROMISING** = at least one peer-reviewed or careful study with quantitative support, not yet replicated or with narrow scope; **SPECULATIVE** = position papers, single unreviewed preprints, or my inference.

Caveat on sources: many of the 2025–2026 student-simulation results are arXiv preprints (marked "preprint"). DOIs were checked in OpenAlex; arXiv IDs were checked by fetching the abstract page.

---

## 1. Pre-LLM learner simulation (SimStudent, simulated students for ITS design, BKT/PFA/DKT simulators, IRT synthetic respondents): what were they good for?

### Takeaway
Pre-LLM simulated learners were useful for narrow engineering jobs: authoring tutors, sanity-checking algorithms, and running psychometric simulation studies where the model generating the data is known by construction. They were not a reliable stand-in for real learners when used to pick instructional policies: policies tuned on a simulator repeatedly looked better than they turned out to be with real students. Almost half of simulated-learner studies from 2010–19 never checked whether the simulator was valid.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED (historical).** VanLehn, Ohlsson & Nason (1994) set out the original uses of simulated students: practice for teachers, formative evaluation of instructional designs, and serving as "classmates" in collaborative settings. They framed simulated students as a design and evaluation tool, not as evidence about learners. — [VanLehn, Ohlsson & Nason 1994, "Applications of simulated students: an exploration", J. AI in Education (OpenAlex W1496771489)](http://www.pitt.edu/~vanlehn/distrib/journal/JAIED94.pdf)
- **ESTABLISHED.** SimStudent is a machine-learning agent that learns production rules from demonstration and tutoring. It was used as an *authoring* tool for Cognitive Tutors, and tutoring SimStudent made authoring more effective. The use was engineering, not surrogate data. — [Matsuda, Cohen & Koedinger 2015, IJAIED, doi:10.1007/s40593-014-0020-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-014-0020-1)
- **ESTABLISHED.** A systematic review of simulated learners in AIED from 2010–19 found that they "tend to represent only narrow aspects of student learning" and that "almost half of the studies using simulated learners do not provide any evidence that their modeling addresses the most fundamental question in simulation design – is the model valid?" The authors propose a Turing-like test as a universal validity criterion. — [Käser & Alexandron 2023/24, IJAIED, doi:10.1007/s40593-023-00337-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-023-00337-2)
- **ESTABLISHED (key negative result).** Instructional policies were evaluated in a simulator built from the same student model used to derive them. This "can overestimate the performance of new teaching methods". In the fractions-tutor case the simulator predicted the adaptive policy would win, but "in an experiment with real students, there was no significant difference between the performance of students taught by the two policies". Doroudi et al.'s Robust Evaluation Matrix evaluates each policy under several *mismatched* student models, and it would have correctly anticipated the null result. — [Doroudi, Aleven & Brunskill 2017, L@S, ERIC ED577159](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED577159.pdf); review of RL instructional sequencing: [Doroudi, Aleven & Brunskill 2019, IJAIED, doi:10.1007/s40593-019-00187-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40593-019-00187-x)
- **PROMISING (2026 preprint, same lesson).** Simulated learner models trained on a dataset of 5,000 learners disagreed on tutor-policy rankings, with a minimum agreement of 0.27. A certification rule that recommends a policy only when all models agree certified just 3.75% of 1,280 settings. The author states the result makes "no claims about actual human learning". — [Kadir 2026, "RankCert", arXiv:2609.26069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26069)
- **ESTABLISHED.** BKT, PFA/AFM and logistic learner models are the standard learner-modelling families. Much of the model-comparison literature uses data simulated from a known generative model to check parameter recovery and metric behaviour. This works because the ground truth is known by construction. — [Pelánek 2017, UMUAI, doi:10.1007/s11257-017-9193-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-017-9193-2)
- **ESTABLISHED.** In psychometrics and statistics, synthetic respondents drawn from a *specified* model are used to evaluate methods, not to learn about people. The ADEMP framework (Aims, Data-generating mechanisms, Estimands, Methods, Performance measures) is the accepted reporting standard for such simulation studies. — [Morris, White & Crowther 2019, Stat Med, doi:10.1002/sim.8086](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.8086)

### Inferences
- The legitimate pre-LLM use was "known-truth simulation": generate data from an explicit model, then check that a pipeline, estimator or tutor behaves correctly on it. The project's surrogate cohort is most defensible in exactly this role, for instrument debugging and coding-pipeline checks. It is least defensible as a substitute for learner data.
- The Doroudi result is the cleanest warning. Even a simulator fitted to real data, when used to choose between interventions, over-predicted the benefit, and the real experiment was null. An LLM surrogate has no fit to real data at all, so the risk is larger.

### Gaps
- I could not verify a citable record of the Ohlsson & Langley 1988 "simulated students" workshop. OpenAlex matched only the 1994 VanLehn–Ohlsson–Nason paper. Cite the 1994 paper instead.
- I did not locate a DKT-based simulator validation with published real-vs-simulated learning-curve fit statistics within the search budget.

## 2. LLM-simulated students (2023–2026): what they can model, the competence paradox, error distributions, item-difficulty correlations

### Takeaway
LLM simulated students can sometimes rank item difficulty moderately to well: r ≈ 0.7–0.8 in favourable, small, often single-model studies. They reproduce *some* distractor preferences only moderately. They fail systematically at the core of what a surrogate learner profile needs: they cannot reliably be *less* competent in a controlled, profile-consistent way (the competence paradox); they drop a simulated misconception whenever given any corrective feedback (near-zero misconception faithfulness); and prompts do not transfer across models. Nothing published shows that prompted personas such as "procedural-without-conceptual" or "brittle heuristics" reproduce the real error distribution of such learners.

### Cited Findings

**Item difficulty and response correctness (the best case)**
- **PROMISING.** Generative Students: 45 GPT-4 "students" defined by knowledge components (mastered, confused, or no evidence) answered 20 MCQs on heuristic evaluation. Per-question correctness correlated with real students at **Pearson r = 0.72**. Random simulated students correlated at **r = −0.16**. There was "considerable overlap" in the hard questions (3 of the hard questions overlapped). The real-student data came from a single course, and the authors report the real data had low internal consistency (0.042). — [Lu & Wang 2024, L@S, doi:10.1145/3657604.3662031](https://doi.org/10.1145/3657604.3662031); [arXiv:2405.11591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11591)
- **PROMISING.** A classroom of 4th, 8th or 12th-grade students role-played by open LLMs, with the outcomes fitted by IRT, correlated with NAEP item-level correctness at **r = 0.75 / 0.76 / 0.82**. LLMs were "poor direct judges of problem difficulty". *Weaker* math models (Gemma) predicted real difficulty better than stronger ones (Llama, Qwen). Names stratified by gender and race improved predictions. (Preprint) — [Acquaye et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.09953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09953)
- **PROMISING.** LLM respondents for College Algebra item calibration: "No single LLM mimics human respondents due to narrow proficiency distributions", but an ensemble of 6 LLMs gets closer. Item parameters correlated with human-calibrated ones at **> 0.8** (e.g. GPT-3.5). Augmenting human data raised Spearman from 0.89 to 0.93. — [Liu, Bhandari & Pardos 2024, arXiv:2407.10899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10899) (published version: *British Journal of Educational Technology* 2025 per later citations; not verified here)
- **PROMISING (negative on portability).** A GPT-3.5 prompt simulating varied skill levels worked across science and reading datasets. However, "the prompt does not generalise well to different LLMs, stressing the need for prompt engineering for each model", and CoT rationale quality did not correlate with simulation accuracy. — [Benedetto et al. 2024, Findings of EMNLP, doi:10.18653/v1/2024.findings-emnlp.663](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.663/)
- **ESTABLISHED (negative).** Predicting difficulty from item text alone is hard. In the BEA 2024 shared task (667 retired USMLE MCQs, 17 submitting teams), the best difficulty predictions reportedly beat a DummyRegressor baseline only marginally (RMSE ≈ 0.29 vs 0.31). — [BEA 2024 Shared Task page](https://sig-edu.org/sharedtask/2024); [participant system paper "Item Difficulty and Response Time Prediction with Large Language Models: An Empirical Analysis of USMLE Items", ACL Anthology 2024.bea-1.44](https://aclanthology.org/2024.bea-1.44/). The 0.29 vs 0.31 figure comes from a secondary summary ([doi:10.3390/ai7070249](https://doi.org/10.3390/ai7070249)); check it against the official findings paper.
- **PROMISING (negative).** On a large-scale Reading & Writing test, the best LLM (zero-shot GPT-4.1) reached QWK = 0.578 for difficulty level, *below* a fine-tuned ConvBERT (0.625). "All LLMs struggled to label hard items", and GPT-5.4 tended to *underestimate* difficulty ("treat most items as easy when their own capabilities increase"). (Preprint) — [Wang et al. 2026, arXiv:2607.28634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28634)
- **PROMISING.** Fine-tuning Qwen-3 with LoRA to answer conditioned on ability descriptors reconstructed item characteristic curves that "compete with or outperform" baselines on Grade 6 ELA and BEA 2024, and were especially good for discrimination. (Preprint) — [Ormerod 2026, arXiv:2601.02580](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02580)
- **PROMISING.** *Data-grounded* personas beat theoretical ones. Latent-class personas learned from Eedi interaction logs, used to condition an LLM, improved IRT-difficulty prediction (R² 0.525 → 0.686; MSE 0.367 → 0.274, 5-fold CV). (Preprint) — [Krishnan & Savelka 2026, arXiv:2605.16290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16290)

**Ability matching (the competence paradox)**
- **PROMISING (negative).** 11 LLMs were placed on the NAEP IRT ability scale using 489 items (math and reading; grades 4, 8, 12). Without guidance, strong models "consistently outperform the average student at every grade". Grade-enforcement prompts help inconsistently: "no evaluated model-prompt pair fits the bill across subjects and grades". — [Srivatsa, Maurya & Kochmar 2025, BEA@ACL, arXiv:2507.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08232)
- **SPECULATIVE (conceptual, but names the failure).** The "competence paradox": broadly capable LLMs asked to emulate partially knowledgeable learners produce "unrealistic error patterns and learning dynamics". The proposed fix is an explicit Epistemic State Specification: what the learner can access, how errors are structured, and how state evolves. The paper is a framework, not an evaluation. (Preprint) — [Yuan et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.05473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05473)
- **PROMISING (negative for role-play).** "LLM role-play follows guidance fluently but does not reliably match the competence of the student being imitated". Across 60 real students (chess, L2 writing, math; public datasets), behavioural fidelity F for GPT-5.4 role-play was 0.23 in chess, against 0.51 for a per-student fine-tuned simulator and 0.45 for Maia2. The trained simulator also beats GPT-5.4 on responsiveness to guidance (0.91 vs 0.72). (Preprint, Microsoft) — [Yang et al. 2026, "StudentSim", arXiv:2609.01591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01591)
- **PROMISING (ACL 2026, negative).** On real math tutoring dialogues (Eedi co-author), "prompting strategies for student simulation perform poorly" on linguistic, behavioural and cognitive metrics. SFT and preference optimisation are "much better but still limited". — [Scarlatos, Lee, Woodhead & Lan 2026, "Simulated Students in Tutoring Dialogues: Substance or Illusion?", arXiv:2601.04025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04025)
- **PROMISING.** Another ACL 2026 Findings paper argues that "one LLM does not simulate all students" and assigns different LLMs to ability strata via cognitive diagnosis. This is further evidence that a single prompted model does not span the ability range. — [Que, Liu et al. 2026, Findings of ACL, 2026.findings-acl.302](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.302/)

**Misconceptions and error distributions**
- **PROMISING (negative, highly relevant).** Misconception faithfulness means keeping a misconception-driven belief and revising it *only* when feedback targets that misconception. Across 7 LLMs (4B–120B) the Selective Flip Score was **near zero**: simulators "correct their answers at similarly high rates regardless of feedback relevance". This is a "sycophantic failure mode", where models behave "more like problem-solvers who treat any corrective signal as a cue to abandon the simulated belief". Post-training can raise SFS by up to +0.56. (Preprint) — [Do, Sonkar & Sachan 2026, arXiv:2605.12748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12748)
- **PROMISING (negative trade-off).** MalAlgoPy shows that LLMs fine-tuned on misconception examples learn to replicate the errors, but "training diminishes the model's ability to solve problems correctly, particularly for problem types where the misconceptions are not applicable". Keeping both properties needed careful tuning of the ratio of correct to misconception examples, sometimes as low as 0.25. — [Sonkar et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.12294](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12294)
- **PROMISING (moderate).** On MCQs with real student response distributions, LLM likelihoods correlate only **moderately** with the distractors students choose. When LLMs err, they are more likely than chance to pick the most popular wrong answer. Even so, "there remains a gap between LLM's underlying reasoning process and human cognitive processes". — [Liu, Sonkar & Baraniuk 2025, arXiv:2502.15140](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15140)
- **PROMISING.** Generating distractors is a proxy for modelling wrong reasoning. On math, models follow a misconception-based process. On science items they fall back to "semantic similarity to the correct answer", which is less robust. Anchoring on the correct solution gave a relative gain of only 6.4% in alignment with human distractors. — [Zengaffinen et al. 2026, Findings of EMNLP, arXiv:2603.15547](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15547)
- **PROMISING.** On Eedi, even a specialised personalised-distractor pipeline reached **40.4%** accuracy at predicting the student's distractor (vs 27.8% for CoT) with Claude-3.5-Sonnet. Individual error prediction stays well below reliable. — [MCTS-guided personalized distractor generation, arXiv:2508.11184](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.11184v1)
- **PROMISING.** Synthetic error data built with cycle consistency between misconception and answer improves simulation of misconception-specific wrong answers and misconception inference (MISTAKE). This is evidence that grounding in explicit misconception→answer mappings helps. — [Ross & Andreas 2025, arXiv:2510.11502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11502)
- **PROMISING (physics-specific, directly relevant).** GPT-4 answering the Force Concept Inventory: prompting it "as if it belonged to a different cohort **yielded no variance in responses**", whereas prompting a specific *preconception* (impetus, active force, etc.) "introduced much variance in responses that approximate real human responses on the FCI **in some regards**". — [Kieser, Wulff, Kuhn & Küchemann 2023, Phys. Rev. PER 19, 020150, doi:10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.19.020150](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.19.020150)
- **ESTABLISHED (general).** LLMs show a "hyper-accuracy distortion": when simulating many humans answering factual questions, they answer too correctly, which distorts wisdom-of-crowds replications. — [Aher, Arriaga & Kalai 2023, ICML, arXiv:2208.10264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10264)

**Interaction and teacher-training use**
- **PROMISING.** GPTeach used GPT-based simulated students for TA training in a user study at L@S 2023. This is a *practice* use (rehearsal), where fidelity matters less than plausibility. — [Markel, Opferman, Landay & Piech 2023, L@S, doi:10.1145/3573051.3593393](https://doi.org/10.1145/3573051.3593393)

### Inferences
- The positive difficulty results (r ≈ 0.72–0.82) have four things in common: (a) *aggregate* item-level correctness, not individual errors; (b) a proficiency spread induced by prompting or ensembling; (c) often *weaker* or ability-conditioned models; (d) validation against real response data that already existed. None showed that a named profile such as "known misconception X" produces the *specific* error that real learners with X produce, at the rate they produce it.
- The competence paradox and the sycophancy/SFS result together imply that profiles such as "known misconception", "brittle heuristics" and "procedural-without-conceptual" will be the *least* faithful part of a prompted surrogate cohort. They will look plausible in single static answers and collapse under probing or feedback, which is exactly the setting of a diagnostic interview.
- The Kieser FCI result suggests physics error variance comes only from what the prompt explicitly specifies. A surrogate cohort therefore reproduces the misconception taxonomy you put in. It cannot tell you the *prevalence* of misconceptions, and cannot discover new ones.
- Per-model prompt brittleness (Benedetto) plus fast model turnover (GPT-3.5 → GPT-5.4 across these papers) means any validation result expires when the model changes.

### Gaps
- I found no study validating LLM-simulated *physics* learners against real error-category distributions on open-response (not MCQ) conservation problems. That is the project's K0 setting.
- I found no validated simulation of the profile types "conceptual-without-transfer" or "intermediate". Treat these as untested.
- Exact r values from the Liu, Sonkar & Baraniuk 2025 distractor-correlation study were not extracted (abstract says only "moderate").
- Mollick's writing on AI personas was not located in a citable scholarly form. I omit it.

## 3. Silicon sampling and LLM survey respondents: the critiques

### Takeaway
Aggregate means and the directions of main effects are often reproduced, sometimes strikingly (r = 0.85 for predicted treatment effects). Variance, subgroup structure, interaction effects, null results and stability over time are not. Synthetic samples systematically under-disperse, which makes power analyses dangerously optimistic. They over-detect effects, and they caricature and flatten subgroups.

### Cited Findings
- **PROMISING (optimistic anchor).** GPT-3 conditioned on real respondents' sociodemographic backstories reproduced subgroup response distributions, a property the authors call "algorithmic fidelity". — [Argyle et al. 2023, Political Analysis, doi:10.1017/pan.2023.2](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2023.2)
- **ESTABLISHED (negative).** ChatGPT synthetic ANES respondents match the means, but "there is less variation in responses than in the real surveys, and regression coefficients often differ significantly". The SD of affective polarisation was 16.1 synthetic vs 31.4 real. A power analysis on synthetic data would suggest about 33 respondents where about 300 are needed. **48%** of coefficients differed significantly, and **32%** of those reversed sign. Identical prompts run in April and July 2023 gave different distributions. — [Bisbee et al. 2024, Political Analysis, doi:10.1017/pan.2024.5](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2024.5)
- **ESTABLISHED.** LM opinions are misaligned with 60 US demographic groups "on par with the Democrat-Republican divide on climate change", and the misalignment "persists even after explicitly steering the LMs towards particular demographic groups". — [Santurkar et al. 2023, ICML, arXiv:2303.17548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17548)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Across 4 LLMs, 3,200 human participants and 16 identities, LLMs "misportray and flatten" demographic groups. Inference-time fixes "reduce, but do not remove, these harms". — [Wang, Morgenstern & Dickerson 2025, Nature Machine Intelligence, doi:10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z)
- **ESTABLISHED.** GPT-4 simulations of certain demographics and of general, uncontroversial topics are "highly susceptible to caricature", measured as individuation and exaggeration. — [Cheng, Piccardi & Yang 2023, EMNLP, doi:10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.669](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.669)
- **PROMISING.** Persona variables explain **< 10%** of annotation variance in subjective NLP datasets, so persona prompting has little to work with. Where they matter, a 70B model captures 81% of the variance achievable by regression on the ground truth. — [Hu & Collier 2024, ACL, arXiv:2402.10811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10811)
- **PROMISING (optimistic, strongest positive).** GPT-4 simulated responses to 70 pre-registered US survey experiments (476 treatment effects, 105,165 participants). Predicted effects correlated with actual effects at **r = 0.85**, and at **r = 0.90** for unpublished studies that could not be in the training data, matching or beating human forecasters. — [Ashokkumar, Hewitt, Ghezae & Willer 2026, Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x); summary: [Stanford AI4PB](https://ai4pb.stanford.edu/projects/predicting-results-of-social-science-experiments-using-large-language-models)
- **ESTABLISHED (negative on interactions and nulls).** In a replication of 156 scenario-based psychology and management experiments with 3 LLMs, GPT-4 replicated **72.7%** of main effects but only **45.7%** of interaction effects. Main-effect replication fell from 76.8% to 41.5% when race variables were involved. LLMs produced significant effects in **68–83%** of cases where the original found a *null*, and effect sizes (Fisher Z) were about 2–3× those in human studies. 35.3% of studies needed prompt adaptation. — [Cui, Li & Zhou 2025, Nature Computational Science, doi:10.1038/s43588-025-00840-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-025-00840-7); [arXiv:2409.00128](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00128)
- **PROMISING (grounding helps, at a price).** Agents built from 2-hour interviews with 1,052 real Americans predicted their held-out GSS answers at 83% (interview), 82% (survey) and 86% (combined) of participants' own two-week test-retest consistency, against 74% for demographics-only agents. Grounding in *individual* data reduced accuracy disparities across groups. — [Park et al. 2024/2026, arXiv:2411.10109](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109). Originally titled "Generative Agent Simulations of 1,000 People", retitled in v3 "LLM Agents Grounded in Self-Reports Enable General-Purpose Simulation of Individuals".
- **ESTABLISHED (negative).** In a simple game (the 11–20 money request game), LLMs "do not exhibit behavior consistent with humans" and show "inconsistent and idiosyncratic responses". — [Gao, Lee, Burtch & Fazelpour 2025, PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.2501660122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2501660122)
- **PROMISING.** Off-the-shelf LLMs reproduced the directions of effects in a canonical accuracy-perception experiment, but "effect magnitudes and moderation patterns vary across models". (Preprint) — [Hoq & Weninger 2026, arXiv:2604.15329](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15329)
- **PROMISING.** Dillion et al. review when LLMs might replace participants and give caveats. The paper is often cited for GPT-3.5's high agreement with human moral judgements, but that number was not re-verified here. — [Dillion, Tandon, Gu & Gray 2023, TiCS, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008)
- **SPECULATIVE (position).** Five challenges for LLM social simulation: diversity, bias, sycophancy, alienness and generalisation. The authors call the last two the hardest, because they may need "a general theory of how LLMs work". — [Anthis et al. 2025, ICML position paper, arXiv:2504.02234](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02234)
- **PROMISING.** Survey of related work in the same space: [Kozlowski & Evans 2025, Sociological Methods & Research, doi:10.1177/00491241251337316](https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241251337316)

### Inferences
- The social-science pattern maps directly onto the education setting. Surrogates are best at the *direction of large, well-documented main effects* and worst at variance, interactions (such as profile × item-type), nulls and minority subgroups. Diagnosis of learner bottlenecks consists almost entirely of interactions (which learner fails which item, and why), so it sits in the weak zone.
- The over-detection of effects that are null in humans (68–83%) is the most dangerous property for a project with falsification gates. A surrogate cohort is biased *toward* passing gates.
- Even the strongest positive result (Ashokkumar et al.) predicts *treatment-effect direction and size for vignette survey experiments*. It does not predict the error patterns of learners on unseen physics items.

### Gaps
- Exact Argyle et al. fidelity statistics were not extracted.
- No education-domain replication of the Bisbee variance analysis was found, i.e. whether the SD of scores in a synthetic class is smaller than in a real one. Inferred likely from the Liu/Pardos finding of a "narrow proficiency distribution" and the Kieser "no variance" finding.

## 4. Persona and role-play fidelity; simulated experts (multi-agent debate, diversity collapse, the silicon crowd, grounded perspectives, STORM, LLM Delphi)

### Takeaway
Multi-agent "expert panels" mostly add ensembling, not independent perspectives. Debate gains are largely explained by majority voting. Interaction tends to collapse diversity, and LLM Delphi panels reach consensus *more* often than human experts, including where humans disagree. Aggregating *different models* gives genuine accuracy gains for forecasting. Perspective-guided questioning (STORM) broadens coverage of *documented* knowledge. Neither shows that simulated experts surface the tacit, undocumented knowledge the project cares about.

### Cited Findings
- **PROMISING (original claim).** Multiple LLM instances debating over rounds improved math and strategic reasoning and factuality. — [Du et al. 2023/ICML 2024, arXiv:2305.14325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325)
- **ESTABLISHED (negative).** Multi-agent debate systems "do not reliably outperform" self-consistency and multi-path ensembling. They are hyperparameter-sensitive and hard to optimise. — [Smit et al. 2024, ICML, arXiv:2311.17371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)
- **ESTABLISHED (negative).** "Majority Voting … accounts for most of the performance gains typically attributed to MAD". Debate is formally a martingale over beliefs, so debate alone does not improve expected correctness. — [Choi et al. 2025, NeurIPS, arXiv:2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17536)
- **PROMISING (negative).** "Persona Collapse" across 10 LLMs: agents given distinct profiles converge on a narrow behavioural mode. Variation "tracks coarse demographic stereotypes rather than the fine-grained individual differences specified in each persona". "The models achieving the highest per-persona fidelity consistently produce the most stereotyped populations." (Preprint) — [Xiao et al. 2026, arXiv:2604.24698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24698)
- **PROMISING (negative).** In multi-agent ideation, stronger, more aligned models yield diminishing diversity. Authority-driven dynamics suppress diversity, and dense communication "accelerate[s] premature convergence". The authors recommend "preserving independence and disagreement". — [Chen et al. 2026, Findings of ACL, arXiv:2604.18005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18005)
- **PROMISING (positive, with caveats).** An ensemble of 12 LLMs on 31 binary questions was "not statistically different" from a crowd of 925 human forecasters. It showed an acquiescence bias (mean predictions significantly above 50% despite an even split of outcomes). Exposure to the human median improved the models by 17–28%, but that was still worse than simply averaging human and machine forecasts. — [Schoenegger et al. 2024, Science Advances, doi:10.1126/sciadv.adp1528](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adp1528)
- **PROMISING (negative for simulated expert disagreement).** In an LLM Delphi study (8 LLMs, 135 obesity-surgery statements, 3 rounds), LLMs reached consensus more often than human experts (**93.3% vs 81.5%**, p = 0.002), including on statements where humans did not agree, "particularly in areas of controversy". 117 of 135 statements reached consensus in round 1, before any interaction. — [Park, Jeon et al. 2025, Int. J. Surgery, doi:10.1097/JS9.0000000000003631](https://doi.org/10.1097/JS9.0000000000003631). A pathology Delphi replication likewise found that all LLM configurations reached consensus more often than the human panel. — [PMC13185839](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185839/). In laboratory medicine, LLM agreement with an expert Delphi was slight with open prompts (κ ≈ 0.25) and moderate with specific ones (κ 0.50–0.52). — [doi:10.1515/dx-2025-0144](https://doi.org/10.1515/dx-2025-0144)
- **PROMISING.** STORM discovers perspectives from similar topics, then simulates writers with those perspectives questioning a *retrieval-grounded* expert. Its articles were judged organised 25% more often and broad in coverage 10% more often (absolute) than a retrieval-plus-outline baseline. — [Shao et al. 2024, NAACL, arXiv:2402.14207](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

### Inferences
- Simulated experts drawing on one pre-training corpus share its priors. "Diversity" from persona prompts is mostly stereotype-level (persona collapse), and interaction reduces it further. LLM panels therefore *manufacture consensus* rather than reveal the genuine disagreement a real expert panel would show.
- Retrieval-grounded agents with *different* corpora (STORM-style) are the most defensible way to get varied perspectives. What they surface is bounded by what is written down. By the project's own framing, tacit expert knowledge is what is *not* written down, so simulated experts can at most propose candidate operations to probe for, never evidence that experts use them.
- Cross-model ensembles (Schoenegger) give real independent-error benefits. If multiple "experts" are used, they should be different model families that do not see each other's answers, aggregated by voting, not by debate.

### Gaps
- I found no study testing whether retrieval-grounded agents with *disjoint* corpora produce disagreement that matches real inter-expert disagreement. This is untested.
- I found no study of LLM-simulated *physics experts* in cognitive task analysis or think-aloud settings.

## 5. Validation methodology: how to validate a simulated learner, and what standards exist

### Takeaway
There is no accepted standard. The field is converging on several distinct checks, each of which must be passed for the use it licenses: (1) distributional match of item difficulty or correctness against real data, with correlation *and* a baseline comparison; (2) variance and ability-range match; (3) error and distractor distribution match; (4) profile coherence, meaning the misconception fires only where applicable; (5) interactive misconception faithfulness under targeted vs control feedback; (6) learning-curve match; (7) blinded Turing-like indistinguishability; and (8) stability across reruns and models. Most published simulators are validated on one or two of these at most.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED.** About half of simulated-learner studies from 2010–19 give no validity evidence. The proposed universal criterion is a Turing-like test. — [Käser & Alexandron 2023/24, doi:10.1007/s40593-023-00337-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-023-00337-2)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Evaluate a policy or instrument under *several mismatched* learner models (Robust Evaluation Matrix). Agreement across models is required before trusting a simulated conclusion. — [Doroudi et al. 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED577159.pdf)
- **PROMISING.** Place simulators on the same IRT ability scale as real students using released NAEP item statistics. — [Srivatsa et al. 2025, arXiv:2507.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08232); [Acquaye et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.09953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09953)
- **PROMISING.** Two-part fidelity: behavioural fidelity F (matching a real individual's responses) and guidance responsiveness R, fit and evaluated on the same public learner records (60 students; chess, L2 writing, math). — [StudentSimEval, Yang et al. 2026, arXiv:2609.01591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01591)
- **PROMISING.** Selective Flip Score: flips under feedback targeting the misconception, minus flips under misaligned-misconception and generic-feedback controls. — [Do et al. 2026, arXiv:2605.12748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12748)
- **PROMISING.** Dual property for misconception models: reproduce the error where the misconception applies, and stay correct where it does not. — [Sonkar et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.12294](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12294)
- **PROMISING.** Linguistic, behavioural and cognitive metrics, with human evaluation on real tutoring dialogues. — [Scarlatos et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.04025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04025)
- **PROMISING.** Population-level metrics for persona sets: Coverage, Uniformity and Complexity against human baselines. — [Xiao et al. 2026, arXiv:2604.24698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24698)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Check variance, coefficient recovery and temporal stability, not only means. — [Bisbee et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2024.5)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Report simulation studies with ADEMP (aims, data-generating mechanism, estimands, methods, performance measures). — [Morris et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.8086)
- **PROMISING.** Contamination control: validate on items or studies that could not be in training data. Ashokkumar et al. used unpublished studies (r = 0.90). — [Ashokkumar et al. 2026, Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x)

### Inferences
- A Turing-like test alone is insufficient. Fluent, plausible text passes it, and fluency is exactly the surface realism that the competence paradox and SFS results show can coexist with wrong underlying behaviour. It must be paired with distributional and interactive tests.
- Every validation is specific to model and version (Benedetto; Bisbee temporal drift), so the model identifier and date have to be frozen into the validation record.

### Gaps
- No agreed numerical thresholds exist (for example, what item-difficulty ρ counts as "valid"). Any threshold the project sets is its own choice and should be pre-registered.

## 6. Ethics and epistemics: the risk that synthetic participants are mistaken for evidence, and how to label them

### Takeaway
The main risk is epistemic, not only ethical. Synthetic data looks like evidence, inflates confidence, narrows the questions asked (monocultures), and can quietly substitute for the learners and experts whose absence it papers over. The literature recommends explicit labelling, transparency about model, prompt and date, supplementing rather than replacing participants, and never drawing inferences about populations from surrogates alone.

### Cited Findings
- **ESTABLISHED (widely cited argument).** "AI as Surrogate" is one of four visions that make scientists vulnerable to "illusions of understanding", including an "illusion of exploratory breadth" (believing one explores all hypotheses when only AI-testable ones are explored). These feed scientific monocultures. — [Messeri & Crockett 2024, Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0)
- **PROMISING (argument).** AI Surrogates risk "illusions of generalizability, where we believe our findings are more generalizable than they actually are". — [Crockett & Messeri 2025, TiCS, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2025.09.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.09.012)
- **PROMISING (argument).** Substitution proposals "conflict with foundational values of work with human participants: representation, inclusion, and understanding". — [Agnew et al. 2024, CHI, doi:10.1145/3613904.3642703](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642703)
- **ESTABLISHED.** Replacement is harmful where identity matters. Use surrogates only to *supplement*. — [Wang et al. 2025, NMI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z)
- **ESTABLISHED.** The authors give practical guidelines on transparency and replicability for LLM-surrogate studies. — [Gao et al. 2025, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2501660122)
- **SPECULATIVE.** The epistemic-fidelity-over-surface-realism framing, with ethical risks, comes from a framework paper. — [Yuan et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.05473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05473)

### Inferences
- For this project, the specific failure is gate contamination. The registered rules K0 (≥ 30% principle-selection or representation errors), K1 (AI-probe-added operations, contradicted-rate CI) and K2 (SESOI d = 0.30) are decided by human data. Any surrogate-derived number near those rules invites anchoring even if it is formally excluded.

### Gaps
- I found no published labelling standard specific to education research, such as a reporting checklist for synthetic learners. The labelling scheme below is my proposal.

## Implications for the project's "evidence-grounded surrogate cohort"

### What a surrogate cohort may legitimately be used for (ordered by strength of support)
1. **Instrument and pipeline debugging** (ESTABLISHED as a use class; known-truth simulation). Generate sessions from an *explicit* specification (the profile's knowledge-component list plus a misconception→answer map) and check that the coding pipeline, codebook and export recover what was put in. This is the pre-LLM "known ground truth" use, and it does not need the surrogate to resemble real learners. It fits `probe-code` and the `codebook-stress-tester` role, which already refuses non-simulated data.
2. **Stress-testing diagnostic items for *logical* discriminability** (PROMISING). Given profile X defined by an explicit misconception, does item Y have *some* response that X would produce and a correct-knowledge learner would not? This tests item design against the project's own taxonomy. It says nothing about prevalence or real difficulty.
3. **Hypothesis and candidate generation** (PROMISING). Candidate misconceptions, distractors and probe questions (MISTAKE; STORM-style perspective questions; retrieval-grounded "expert" agents on *disjoint* corpora, aggregated by independent vote, not debate). Every candidate is a hypothesis to be checked against literature and, later, humans.
4. **Rehearsal and training** (PROMISING). Interviewer rehearsal of probe protocols against simulated experts or students, GPTeach-style, where the goal is practising the procedure, not measuring anything.
5. **Coarse item-difficulty *ranking* screen** (PROMISING, *only after* passing the validation protocol below on a public proxy). Even then, use it only to order pilot items, never to set cut-scores or drop items without human data.

### What it must never be used for
- Deciding or informing any registered gate: K0 error proportions, K1 operation counts or contradicted rates, K2 effect sizes, Stage B arm matching, or the delay. Surrogate outputs over-detect effects (68–83% false positives on human nulls; [Cui et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-025-00840-7)) and under-disperse ([Bisbee et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2024.5)).
- **Power analysis or sample-size planning.** Under-dispersion shrank the implied N from about 300 to about 33 in Bisbee et al.
- Estimating the *prevalence* of any error type, misconception or learner profile in a real cohort.
- Standing in for the 12 Stage A physicists, or claiming an operation is "expert knowledge" because a simulated expert produced it. Simulated panels manufacture consensus ([Park, Jeon et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1097/JS9.0000000000003631)) and cannot access undocumented tacit knowledge.
- Evaluating the diagnostic or elicitation tool's *effectiveness* and choosing between interventions or policies. See the Doroudi fractions-tutor null and [RankCert](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26069) (3.75% certifiable).
- Validating any profile under interaction (feedback, probing) without an SFS-style test. The default is near-zero faithfulness ([Do et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12748)).
- Any claim, figure or table in a publication or map entry that is not labelled synthetic.

### Labelling and epistemic-status rule (proposed)
Every surrogate artefact carries: `SYNTHETIC — not evidence about learners or experts`, together with the model ID and version, the date, the prompt or spec hash, the profile spec, and the validation tier reached (T0 = unvalidated; T1–T4 as below). Surrogate outputs live in a separate directory from study data and are never merged into `sessions/`. Assumptions A1–A10 stay "untested" regardless of any surrogate result.

### Concrete validation protocol using only PUBLIC real-learner data (zero new participants)
Pre-register the metrics, thresholds and baselines below before running. Freeze the model ID and date. Rerun the full protocol on any model change.

**Data (public).**
- NAEP released item-level statistics, as used in [Srivatsa et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08232) and [Acquaye et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09953), for ability and difficulty.
- Eedi, for misconception-labelled distractors and real response distributions ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15140); [Krishnan & Savelka 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16290)).
- ASSISTments, EdNet and PSLC DataShop for learning curves and KC models. DataShop includes physics-tutor datasets; which ones are public must be checked.
- Published FCI response-pattern data, where available, for the physics sanity check ([Kieser et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.19.020150)).
- Contamination control: hold out items released after the model's training cutoff, or with no public answer key, where possible.

**Tiers (each licenses only the uses listed).**
- **T1, ability and variance (licenses nothing on its own).** Fit IRT to real plus simulated responses on the common scale. Pass only if (a) the simulated ability distribution covers the real 10th–90th percentile range, and (b) the SD ratio of simulated to real total scores is at least about 0.7. Compare against Bisbee's ≈ 0.5.
- **T2, item difficulty (licenses use 5).** Spearman ρ between simulated and real item p-values or IRT b on held-out items. Pass requires ρ ≥ a pre-set value (the literature's upper range is 0.72–0.82) *and* beating two baselines by a pre-set margin: a text-feature regressor and a direct "rate the difficulty" LLM prompt. The baseline comparison matters because of the BEA 2024 near-baseline results.
- **T3, error and distractor distribution (required before any profile is described as "realistic").** Per item, Jensen–Shannon divergence between simulated and real distractor distributions, plus top-distractor agreement, on Eedi items with labelled misconceptions. Compare against a uniform-over-distractors null and a "most-plausible-distractor" LLM baseline. For each named profile, the dual-property test: error rate where the misconception applies vs accuracy where it does not ([Sonkar et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12294)).
- **T4, interaction faithfulness (required before surrogates are used in probe or interview rehearsal as anything more than practice).** Selective Flip Score with targeted, misaligned and generic feedback controls. Pass requires SFS clearly above 0.
- **Population checks, run at every tier.** Coverage and uniformity of the profile set relative to real latent classes (LCA on Eedi or ASSISTments). Rerun on ≥ 3 seeds and ≥ 2 model families. Report stability: a result that flips across reruns fails.
- **Learning-curve check (only if surrogates will simulate change over practice).** Fit AFM or BKT on real DataShop or ASSISTments logs and on simulated logs over the same KC model. Compare the learning-curve slope and intercept per KC. Pass requires the same sign and overlapping CIs on most KCs.
- **Blinded Turing-like test ([Käser & Alexandron](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-023-00337-2)), used as a *necessary*, never a sufficient, check.** A domain rater classifies mixed real and simulated responses. Target near-chance discrimination with a pre-set AUC bound.
- **Mismatch robustness ([Doroudi REM](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED577159.pdf)).** Any conclusion drawn from surrogates, such as "item Y discriminates profile X", must hold across ≥ 2 independently built surrogate models, for example a prompted LLM and a data-grounded LCA-persona or fine-tuned model. Otherwise it is reported as model-dependent.

**Default expectation, from the literature.** A prompted-only cohort will likely pass T1 only with ensembling or ability conditioning. It may pass T2 on aggregate difficulty. It will likely fail T3 at the profile level and fail T4 (SFS ≈ 0). The cohort should therefore be planned for uses 1–4, with use 5 treated as contingent on results.

**What would change this assessment.** A published, replicated demonstration on open-response physics items that profile-conditioned simulators match real error-category distributions (T3) and keep misconceptions under targeted vs control feedback (T4) would move profile-level use from "never" to "screening only, after local validation". The data-grounded, trained simulators (StudentSim, SFS-aligned RL, LCA personas) are the direction most likely to produce such evidence. Prompted personas are the least likely.
