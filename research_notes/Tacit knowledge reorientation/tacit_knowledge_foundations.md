# Tacit / omitted expert knowledge: foundations, elicitation, and what is reconstructable without observing humans

Evidence tags: **ESTABLISHED** (replicated or meta-analytic, or a foundational result not seriously contested), **PROMISING** (a few supporting studies, small n, or a single research group), **SPECULATIVE** (theory, argument, single preprint, or my own inference). "Verified" means the DOI was resolved through OpenAlex on 2026-09-28 and the title plus first author matched. Where a claim rests on a source I did not re-open in this session it says "(content not re-read)".

## 1. Why experts omit knowledge: expert blind spot, curse of knowledge, automaticity, and the "70%" claim

### Takeaway
That experts leave things out when they explain unaided is **ESTABLISHED**. The usual mechanism given is proceduralisation/automaticity, plus a separate mis-calibration about what novices find hard. The specific "experts omit ~70% of decision steps" figure is **weakly supported**: it rests on a handful of very small studies (n = 3–6 experts each), mostly from one group (USC: Clark, Feldon, Yates, Sullivan), and omission is measured against gold standards that were themselves built by CTA. Treat "70%" as a slogan, not a parameter.

### Cited Findings
- **Primary evidence behind "70%" (Chao & Salvendy 1994).** Six expert programmers did troubleshooting tasks with all actions recorded, then explained their procedures under several elicitation methods. "No single expert was able to report more than 41% of their diagnostic actions, 53% of their debugging actions, or 29% of their interpretations, regardless of the knowledge elicitation method used." Aggregating all six experts raised coverage to 87%, 88% and 62%. **PROMISING** (n = 6, one domain). As reported in [Clark, Feldon, van Merriënboer, Yates & Early 2008, CTA chapter, pp. 584–585](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf); primary: [Chao & Salvendy 1994, IJHCI 6(2)](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447319409526093).
- **Sullivan et al. 2014 (surgery, cricothyrotomy).** Three experts were videotaped teaching; the gold-standard task list was built by CTA with three *other* surgeons. Experts omitted on average 71% (10/14) of clinical-knowledge steps, 51% (14/27) of action steps and 73% (3.6/5) of decision steps. They described "how to do it" for only 13% (3.6/27) of action steps. CTA probing raised coverage from 44% (20/46) of steps unprompted to 66% (31/46) prompted. **PROMISING** (n = 3, one procedure; the denominator is analyst-defined and CTA-derived, hence partly circular). [Sullivan et al. 2014, Acad Med 89(5):811–816](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000000224).
- Companion integrated studies on central venous catheter placement and open cricothyrotomy from the same group: [Yates, Sullivan & Clark 2012, Am J Surg 203(1):76](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjsurg.2011.07.011) (exact omission numbers not re-read).
- **Where the round number comes from.** The Clark et al. chapter states that "experts are not fully aware of about 70% of their own decisions and mental analysis of tasks (Clark and Elen, 2006; Feldon and Clark, 2006)". Both citations are review chapters by the same authors, not new data. The data trail ends at Chao & Salvendy 1994, Feldon's 2004 USC dissertation (research psychologists describing experimental-design strategies; numbers not retrieved) and the USC surgical studies. — [Clark et al. 2008 chapter](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).
- **The strongest single datum for "text is incomplete".** Crandall & Getchell-Reiter (1993) ran Critical Decision Method interviews with 17 NICU nurses (mean 13 years' experience, 8.1 years specialised). CDM elicited significantly more information than non-CDM interviews. Of the 70 cues the nurses used to detect infant distress, **25 (over one third) were absent from the medical and nursing training literature of the time**, spanning seven previously unrecognised categories that were then incorporated into novice training. **PROMISING** (one domain, 1993). [Crandall & Getchell-Reiter 1993, Adv Nurs Sci 16(1):42](https://doi.org/10.1097/00012272-199309000-00006); numbers as reported in [Clark et al. 2008, p. 584](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).
- **Expertise diverges from declared policy.** Neuropsychologists' IQ estimates diverged from the predictor relationships they themselves had articulated, many being uncorrelated with them (Kareken & Williams 1994). Physicians' self-reports listed long symptom lists, but only 1–4 symptoms actually drove their diagnoses (Einhorn 1974); expert–expert agreement on identical cases ran r ≈ .40–.50. **ESTABLISHED** as a family of findings in judgement research; individual studies are small. Summarised in [Clark et al. 2008, pp. 583–584](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).
- **Written instructions omit universally practised steps.** An expert engineering team repeatedly failed to produce a complete set of assembly instructions for research equipment; the missing step was a practice so universal among them that no one thought to write it (Collins et al. 1985). **Anecdotal / SPECULATIVE**. [Clark et al. 2008, p. 584](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).
- **Mechanism: knowledge compilation (ACT/ACT-R).** Skill moves from a declarative stage in which facts are interpreted, through knowledge compilation (composition and proceduralisation), into production rules that no longer retrieve the declarative facts. This predicts that verbalisable intermediate steps drop out with practice. **ESTABLISHED** as theory. [Anderson 1982, Psych Rev 89(4):369](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.89.4.369).
- **Automaticity in the CTA literature.** Automated knowledge runs outside awareness and faster than conscious processes, so it is "not available for introspection or accurate self-monitoring", and automated processes are largely uninterruptible. Unaided self-reports are therefore "typically inaccurate or incomplete". Newly generated elements of a genuinely novel problem are reported more accurately than pre-existing routines applied to it (Betsch et al. 1998). [Clark et al. 2008, p. 583](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf); [Feldon 2007, "Cognitive Load and Classroom Teaching: The Double-Edged Sword of Automaticity", Educ Psychol 42(3):123](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701416173); [Feldon 2007, Educ Psych Rev 19:91](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-006-9009-0). **ESTABLISHED** in direction, uncertain in size.
- **Attending to automated skill degrades it.** Directing experienced golfers and soccer players to attend to skill execution impaired performance, whereas divided attention did not; novices showed the reverse. **ESTABLISHED** for sensorimotor skill. [Beilock, Carr, MacMahon & Starkes 2002, JEP:Applied 8(1):6](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.8.1.6) (content not re-read).
- **Limits of self-report generally.** People often cannot report the causes of their own behaviour; their reports match the predictions of *observers* holding the same cultural causal theories, suggesting the reports come from shared a-priori theories rather than from introspection. **ESTABLISHED** (with qualifications). [Nisbett & Wilson 1977, Psych Rev 84(3):231](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231) (content not re-read). Counterpoint: concurrent verbalisation of *heeded* information is a valid trace. [Ericsson & Simon 1980, Psych Rev 87(3):215](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.87.3.215).
- **Think-aloud is non-reactive; asking for explanations is not.** A meta-analysis of 94 studies (~3,500 participants) found the think-aloud effect on accuracy "indistinguishable from zero (r = −.03)", robust to task type; procedures that require describing or explaining thoughts were significantly reactive and *raised* performance above silent controls; all verbal-report procedures increased task completion time. **ESTABLISHED**. [Fox, Ericsson & Best 2011, Psych Bull 137(2):316](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021663).
- **Expert blind spot (education).** Preservice secondary teachers (N = 48) with more advanced mathematics education were more likely to treat symbolic-equation mastery as a prerequisite for word and story problems, "in contrast with students' actual performance patterns"; the same pattern appeared across mathematics, science and language arts. **ESTABLISHED/PROMISING**. [Nathan & Petrosino 2003, AERJ 40(4):905](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312040004905).
- **Curse of expertise (prediction).** Across two studies, one with expertise experimentally manipulated, more expert participants were *worse* at predicting novice task completion times and were resistant to debiasing; intermediates were most accurate. **ESTABLISHED/PROMISING**. [Hinds 1999, JEP:Applied 5(2):205](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.5.2.205).
- **Expert instructions are more abstract, which cuts both ways.** In an electronic circuit-wiring task, experts used more abstract and advanced statements and fewer concrete ones. Novices taught by beginners outperformed novices taught by experts on the target task, but expert-taught novices did better on a *different* task in the same domain. **PROMISING**. [Hinds, Patterson & Pfeffer 2001, J Appl Psych 86(6):1232](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.86.6.1232).
- **Pooling experts reduces omission.** Aggregation raised coverage sharply in Chao & Salvendy, and the CTA literature accordingly recommends two or more SMEs interviewed separately to avoid premature consensus. **PROMISING**. [Clark et al. 2008, pp. 579, 585](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).

### Inferences
- Omission has two separable sources that matter very differently for reconstruction. (i) **Automated procedural/perceptual steps**: the expert has the knowledge but cannot verbalise it (Anderson; Beilock; Feldon). (ii) **Mis-modelled novice difficulty**: the expert cannot see which steps are hard (Nathan & Petrosino; Hinds). Source (ii) is a fact about *learners*, not about experts, and is best recovered from learner performance data rather than from expert text or expert interviews.
- Nisbett & Wilson imply that ordinary expert *self-explanation* is partly generated from culturally shared theories of the task. An LLM trained on public text should therefore be expected to reproduce what experts **say** they do rather than what they **do**. Reconstruction should be benchmarked against CTA-elicited or trace-derived knowledge, never against unaided expert explanation, or the benchmark will be trivially easy.
- The Chao & Salvendy aggregation result is the single most encouraging empirical fact for reconstruct-first: pooling many independent expert explanations, which is roughly what a language model does over a literature, recovers far more than any one expert. The residual it misses is concentrated in "interpretations" (62% even pooled) — i.e., judgement, not steps.

### Gaps
- Feldon's 2004 dissertation numbers and the Feldon & Clark 2006 chapter were not retrieved; the "70%" chain beyond Chao & Salvendy and the USC surgical studies is unverified.
- No independent-lab replication of a quantified omission rate measured against a *behavioural-trace* gold standard (rather than a CTA-derived task list) was found. This is the key missing study, and it is the study this project's Stage A is closest to.

## 2. Decoding the Disciplines (DtD) — one option among several

### Takeaway
DtD is a faculty-development/SoTL paradigm: identify a student "bottleneck", have colleagues from *other* disciplines interview the expert to surface the missing mental operations, then model, practise, assess and share them. It is widely adopted and has broadened considerably since 2004, but its evidence base is case studies and interview analyses. I found no controlled trial of DtD's effect on learning. **PROMISING as a procedure, weakly evidenced as an intervention.**

### Cited Findings
- **Origin.** A seven-step model (bottleneck → how experts do it → model → practice/feedback → motivation → assessment → sharing), presented as "a model for helping students learn disciplinary ways of thinking". [Middendorf & Pace 2004, New Directions for Teaching and Learning 2004(98):1](https://doi.org/10.1002/tl.142) (324 citations); process description at [Indiana University CITL](https://citl.indiana.edu/teaching-resources/course-design/decoding-disciplines/index.html).
- **Scope expansion ("Decoding 2.0").** Pace writes that the paradigm "has undergone enormous changes since its appearance in 2004", expanding to "emotional, bodily, and social learning", creating new roles for students, and exploring "learning beyond the individual". **ESTABLISHED as description of the field**. [Pace 2021, Teaching & Learning Inquiry 9(2)](https://doi.org/10.20343/teachlearninqu.9.2.3).
- **Typical evidence form.** Multidisciplinary faculty learning communities report their bottlenecks with inductive content analysis of the Decoding interviews (e.g., seven faculty members). [Boman et al. 2017, NDTL 2017(150)](https://doi.org/10.1002/tl.20234); [Miller-Young & Boman 2017, NDTL 148 (ERIC EJ1144576)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1144576). Book-length treatment: [Pace 2017, *The Decoding the Disciplines Paradigm*, Indiana University Press](https://openalex.org/W2616880012).

### Inferences
- The "decoding interview" is structurally a lightweight CTA run by a non-expert interviewer; unlike CDM or PARI it has no outcome meta-analysis behind it. Its distinctive asset is the **bottleneck-first** framing, which aims elicitation only at what learners actually fail at. That framing is highly compatible with reconstruct-first, because bottlenecks can often be located from existing student-error data before any expert is interviewed.

### Gaps
- No RCT or quasi-experiment isolating DtD's learning effect; no head-to-head comparison of decoding interviews against CDM/PARI for knowledge yield.

## 3. Cognitive Task Analysis: effect sizes, method families, and what each recovers

### Takeaway
CTA-based instruction beats instruction built from experts' unaided descriptions, with large effects (controlled Hedges g ≈ 0.87 across domains; surgery SMD 1.4–2.1). The underlying studies are few and small and often come from the originating groups. The frequently quoted d = 1.72 is a *pre–post* effect from an unpublished dissertation and must not be cited as a between-group advantage. Different method families recover different knowledge; no single method is sufficient.

### Cited Findings
- **Tofel-Grehl & Feldon 2013 (JCEDM).** Meta-analysis of CTA-based instruction versus "other means for identifying and representing instructional content": "Overall, the effect of CTA-based instruction is large (Hedges's g = 0.871). However, effect sizes vary substantially by both CTA method used and training context… Though limited by a relatively small number of studies, the notable effect size indicates that the information elicited through CTA provides a strong basis for highly effective instruction." **PROMISING→ESTABLISHED in direction**. [Tofel-Grehl & Feldon 2013, JCEDM 7(3):293–304](https://doi.org/10.1177/1555343412474821). The NDM Association's index records the methods covered as Critical Decision Method and PARI ([NDMA entry](https://naturalisticdecisionmaking.org/cta-literature/cognitive-task-analysis-based-training-a-meta-analysis-of-studies/)).
- **Lee 2004 (dissertation) — the source of "d = 1.72".** A ten-database search returned 318 studies; **7 qualified**, yielding 39 comparisons of *pre- versus post-test* differences, effect sizes .91–2.45, mean **d = +1.72**, "overall percentage of post-training performance gain was 75.2%" (χ² = 6.50, p < .01). Because the comparisons are within-group pre–post rather than CTA versus control, this is not evidence of CTA's *added* value. **SPECULATIVE as a CTA-vs-alternative estimate**. [Clark et al. 2008, p. 586](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf) reporting Lee, R. L. (2004), Ed.D. dissertation, USC. Note that secondary sources restate this as "a 46% gain, d = 1.72, compared with traditional expert-based task analysis" ([3-Star Learning Experiences blog](https://3starlearningexperiences.wordpress.com/2020/09/01/cognitive-task-analysis-an-overlooked-gem/)), which **conflicts** with the chapter's description of the design. Prefer Tofel-Grehl & Feldon's g = 0.871 for any controlled claim.
- **Surgery meta-analysis (independent group).** PRISMA review of four databases to February 2021: 2,205 records → 12 studies (RCTs and observational; 7 with surgical trainees, 4 medical students, 1 mixed). CTA-based training versus conventional training gave enhanced procedural knowledge (**SMD 1.36, 95% CI 0.67–2.05, P < 0.001**) and superior technical performance (**SMD 2.06, 95% CI 1.17–2.96, P < 0.001**), random-effects model. **PROMISING** (small studies, wide CIs). [Edwards, Coombs, Szyszka et al. 2021, BJS Open 5(6):zrab122](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjsopen/zrab122).
- **Exemplar controlled studies.** Velmahos et al. (2004), central venous catheter placement: a lecture generated by CTA of two experts beat the expert instructor's free-recall lecture on an observational checklist, number of needle insertion attempts, frequency of required assistance and time to completion, with equal time for questions and practice in both arms. Schaafstal et al. (2000), radar system troubleshooting: the CTA-based course solved "more than twice as many malfunctions, in less time", replicated or exceeded in every later cohort. **PROMISING**. [Clark et al. 2008, pp. 585–586](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).
- **Cost–benefit.** Only one field comparison of traditional task analysis versus cognitive analysis, in a European organisation of 10,000+ employees. **SPECULATIVE** (single case). [Clark & Estes 1996, Int J Educ Res 25(5):403](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0883-0355(97)81235-9).
- **Method families.** Cooke's taxonomy groups elicitation into (a) observations and interviews, (b) process tracing (think-aloud, eye movements, and similar) and (c) conceptual techniques (card sorting, pairwise relatedness ratings, repertory grid, concept mapping) (content not re-read). [Cooke 1994, "Varieties of knowledge elicitation techniques", IJHCS 41(6):801](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1994.1083) (584 citations). Later review and reappraisal of CTA methods for job and task design: [Wei & Salvendy 2004, Behaviour & IT 23(4):273](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290410001673036).
- **Methods map onto knowledge types, and CTA starts with documents.** "Knowledge elicitation methods map directly to knowledge types. Some are best used to elicit procedural knowledge, while others are more successful for capturing declarative knowledge (Chipman et al., 2000)." The CTA process begins with **document analysis** plus observation and unstructured interviews; crucially, "definitive identification of an expert's mental operations cannot be accomplished through observation" — observation only flags *where* decisions occur. [Clark et al. 2008, pp. 579–580](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf).
- **CDM.** A retrospective incident-based interview with multiple sweeps probing cues, goals, options, expectancies and hypotheticals. [Klein, Calderwood & MacGregor 1989, IEEE Trans SMC 19(3):462](https://doi.org/10.1109/21.31053) (1,039 citations). Yield evidence: the NICU study above.
- **ACTA.** A practitioner's toolkit (task diagram, knowledge audit, simulation interview, cognitive demands table) designed to be usable by people without CTA training. [Militello & Hutton 1998, Ergonomics 41(11):1618](https://doi.org/10.1080/001401398186108) (content not re-read).
- **Situation-awareness requirements.** GDTA derives SA requirements from a goal–decision hierarchy across three SA levels (perception, comprehension, projection). [Endsley 1995, Human Factors 37(1):32](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779049543) (8,765 citations); [Endsley, Bolte & Jones 2003, *Designing for Situation Awareness*](https://openalex.org/W1544152542).
- **Foundational references, existence verified.** [Schraagen, Chipman & Shalin (eds.) 2000, *Cognitive Task Analysis*, Erlbaum](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781410605795); [Crandall, Klein & Hoffman 2006, *Working Minds: A Practitioner's Guide to Cognitive Task Analysis*, MIT Press](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/7304.001.0001); [Hoffman & Militello 2008, *Perspectives on Cognitive Task Analysis*, Psychology Press](https://openalex.org/W606130880); [Hoffman, Shadbolt, Burton & Klein 1995, OBHDP 62(2):129](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1995.1039).
- **Knowledge-type ↔ method mapping (my synthesis of the above, not a validated table).** Declarative concepts/principles → interviews, documents, concept maps. Cue and pattern recognition → CDM probes, simulation interviews, contrived limited-information tasks. Procedural sequences → observation plus concurrent think-aloud, PARI-style problem posing. Conceptual structure → card sort, repertory grid, pairwise ratings, laddering. SA requirements → GDTA. **SPECULATIVE as a mapping.**

### Inferences
- The CTA advantage (g ≈ 0.87; surgery SMD 1.4–2.1) is the best available upper bound on the instructional value of recovering omitted knowledge. A reconstruct-first pipeline should be judged by how much of that gap it closes: is instruction built from reconstruction closer to CTA-based instruction or to free-recall-based instruction? That is a three-arm design, and it is cheap relative to a full CTA study.
- CTA itself *begins* with document analysis. Reconstruct-first is not a break with CTA; it proposes to make CTA's phase-1 document analysis far more powerful and thereby shrink the expert-contact phases.

### Gaps
- The Tofel-Grehl & Feldon full text was inaccessible (HTTP 403 on the repository copy). Per-method effect sizes (CDM vs PARI vs other), k, heterogeneity and publication-bias checks are **unverified** here; only the overall g = 0.871 and the statement that effects "vary substantially by CTA method" are confirmed from the abstract.
- No meta-analysis compares *elicitation methods against each other* on instructional outcomes within one domain.

## 4. The knowledge-acquisition bottleneck and the differential access hypothesis

### Takeaway
The expert-systems era established that getting knowledge out of experts, not representing it, was the rate-limiting step. Controlled comparisons found that no single technique captures the full range of knowledge, and that think-aloud protocol analysis was the *least efficient* technique in classification domains. The strong form of differential access (each technique taps a distinct store) is weakly supported; the weak form (techniques are complementary and must be combined) is **ESTABLISHED**.

### Cited Findings
- Feigenbaum framed knowledge engineering and the difficulty of acquiring knowledge from experts as applied AI's central bottleneck. [Feigenbaum 1984, Ann NY Acad Sci 426:91](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.1984.tb16513.x) (content not re-read).
- **Burton, Shadbolt, Rugg & Hedgecock 1990.** Structured interview, protocol analysis, card sort and laddered grid compared across two classification domains with eight experts each. "Despite its common usage, protocol analysis is shown to be the least efficient technique", and no one technique elicited a comprehensive range of knowledge even for routine rule-based systems. **PROMISING** (n = 16, classification tasks only). [Burton et al. 1990, Knowledge Acquisition 2(2):167](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1042-8143(05)80010-X); summarised in [a Maynooth review of KE techniques](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/7052/1/TA-Knowledge-elicitation.pdf).
- The Southampton programme framed the question as "not just the crude question of whether one technique is better or worse than another" but the **complementarity of pairs of techniques**. [Shadbolt, O'Hara & Crow, "The experimental evaluation of knowledge acquisition techniques and methods"](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/252281/1/shadbolt-ohara-crow_experimental_evaluation.pdf).
- A later independent comparison of KE methods: [Jones 1996, Expert Systems 13(4)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0394.1996.tb00125.x) (content not re-read). Shadbolt & Smart's modern retrospective: [Shadbolt & Smart, "Knowledge acquisition and the rise of social machines", IJHCS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581912001607) (content not re-read).
- CommonKADS systematised KE into model-based knowledge engineering (task, inference, domain models). [Schreiber et al. 2000, *Knowledge Engineering and Management: The CommonKADS Methodology*, MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262193009/knowledge-engineering-and-management/) — **DOI not verified**; the OpenAlex match for this title is a review in *Computers & Mathematics with Applications* ([10.1016/S0898-1221(00)90199-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0898-1221(00)90199-8)), not the book itself.
- Repertory grids appear inside CTA practice as a knowledge-representation technique (Bradshaw et al. 1993, cited in [Clark et al. 2008, p. 580](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Complementarity implies that LLM reconstruction is simply one more technique with its own access profile: probably strong where card sorts and laddering are strong (conceptual structure, taxonomies, stated principles) and weak where CDM is strong (episode-specific cue knowledge, expectancies, what would have gone wrong). A reconstruct-first design should therefore expect the human residual to be *cue and expectancy knowledge*, and should point the human protocol precisely there rather than at the whole task.

### Gaps
- No post-2010 replication of the differential-access experiments, and none that includes LLM-generated knowledge as a condition. This is an obvious and cheap study.

## 5. Typologies of tacit knowledge: which kinds are explicable in principle

### Takeaway
Collins's three-way typology is the most useful frame for reconstruct-first. **Relational tacit knowledge** (RTK) is tacit for contingent social reasons — never asked, never written, held back, too costly to spell out — and is *explicable in principle*. **Somatic** (STK) is tied to bodies and brains; parts can be explained scientifically, but explanation does not confer the skill. **Collective** (CTK) inheres in social practice and, for Collins, cannot be made explicit. Most of what the CTA literature calls "omitted" knowledge is RTK plus automated cognitive procedure — exactly the categories a model with public text could partly recover.

### Cited Findings
- Collins identifies three kinds of tacit knowledge — weak, medium and strong — dealing respectively with "the contingencies of social life (relational tacit knowledge), the nature of the human body and brain (somatic tacit knowledge), and the nature of human society (collective tacit knowledge)". In principle RTK is explicable, STK less so, and CTK "impossible". **ESTABLISHED as a framework** (conceptual, not empirical). [Collins 2010, *Tacit and Explicit Knowledge*, University of Chicago Press](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226113821.001.0001) (942 citations); see also the [publisher's description](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/T/bo8461024.html) and a [review essay](https://compositionforum.com/issue/49/amidon-collins-review.php).
- **A critique that sharpens the boundary.** Collins himself explicates part of the somatic knowledge of bike-riding (a scientific explanation of balance), so "STK" as defined mixes explicable and inexplicable content; what makes a piece of knowledge tacit is whether the reason it is unexplicated is contingent or intrinsic. The critique also separates collective knowledge that is untold "for accidental reasons — for reasons that have nothing to do with the nature of the knowledge involved" from collective knowledge inexplicable because of its collective nature. [Philosophia Scientiæ, "Collins's Taxonomy of Tacit Knowledge: Critical Analyses and Possible Extensions"](https://journals.openedition.org/philosophiascientiae/892?lang=en); [preprint copy](https://hal.univ-lorraine.fr/hal-01867441v1/document).
- **Polanyi**: "we can know more than we can tell". [Polanyi 1966, *The Tacit Dimension*](https://openalex.org/W1606540910) (OpenAlex matches a 1997 reprint chapter; 12,454 citations). The economic restatement, "Polanyi's paradox" — tasks whose rules we cannot articulate resist codification — is [Autor 2014, NBER w20485](https://doi.org/10.3386/w20485).
- **Ryle vs intellectualism.** Ryle separated knowing-how from knowing-that ([Ryle 1946, Proc Aristotelian Soc 46:1](https://doi.org/10.1093/aristotelian/46.1.1)); Stanley & Williamson argue knowing-how is a species of knowing-that ([Stanley & Williamson 2001, J Philosophy 98(8):411](https://doi.org/10.2307/2678403)). **Contested, philosophical.** If intellectualism is right, more know-how is propositional and hence textually expressible in principle.
- **Dreyfus skill model.** Five stages from novice to expert; experts act intuitively and no longer follow rules, which Dreyfus used against rule-based AI. [Dreyfus 2004, Bull Sci Tech Soc 24(3):177](https://doi.org/10.1177/0270467604264992) (1,257 citations). **ESTABLISHED as a model; SPECULATIVE as a claim about the limits of AI.**
- **SECI critique.** Nonaka's tacit→explicit "externalisation" ([Nonaka 1994, Org Sci 5(1):14](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.5.1.14), 17,773 citations) is criticised as conceptually confused and empirically unsupported ([Gourlay 2006, J Management Studies 43(7):1415](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2006.00637.x)). Tsoukas argues tacit knowledge is not converted but "punctuated" through dialogue and joint practice ([Tsoukas 2009, Org Sci 20(6):941](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1090.0435)) (content not re-read). **Relevant caution:** the whole idea of a clean "externalisation" step is contested in the field that invented it.
- **Sternberg's tacit-knowledge inventories.** Written scenario-based tests of practical know-how (managing self, others, career) predicted job performance in several studies. [Wagner & Sternberg 1985, JPSP 49(2):436](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.49.2.436); [Sternberg, Wagner, Williams & Horvath 1995, Am Psychologist 50(11):912](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.50.11.912). Criticised as essentially job-knowledge tests whose independence from g is overstated: [Gottfredson 2003, Intelligence 31(4):343](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0160-2896(02)00085-5). **Contested.**

### Inferences
- The Sternberg literature is, for our purposes, evidence that a great deal of "tacit" practical knowledge can be **assessed in writing**: experts reliably recognise good actions in text even when they cannot state rules. That makes written vignettes with expert-rated response options a cheap instrument for testing whether reconstructed knowledge matches expert judgement — a World A/B validation route that needs experts only as raters, for minutes rather than hours.
- In Collins's terms, a language model trained on public text is a very large store of already-*explicated* RTK plus the linguistic traces of collective practice. That predicts high fluency about practice and low reliability about the parts of practice that were never written. The prediction is testable: reconstructed knowledge should match published/derivable content well and match CDM-elicited cue knowledge poorly.

### Gaps
- Collins's interactional-expertise papers and Tsoukas (2003) "Do we really understand tacit knowledge?" were not retrieved; both bear directly on whether a text-trained system can hold usable knowledge of a practice it cannot perform.

## 6. Naturalistic decision making, situation awareness, and work-as-imagined vs work-as-done

### Takeaway
NDM shows that proficient decisions are mostly recognition-primed: situations are recognised via cues, expectancies, plausible goals and typical actions, then mentally simulated. Those are precisely the elements CDM and GDTA target, and precisely the elements least likely to be written down. Safety-II's "work-as-imagined vs work-as-done" is the sharpest available warning against a documents-first strategy.

### Cited Findings
- **RPD and NDM.** Klein's review covers recognition-primed decision making, the role of cues and expectancies, and the CDM. [Klein 2008, "Naturalistic Decision Making", Human Factors 50(3):456](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872008X288385) (2,231 citations); [Klein 1998, *Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions*, MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262611466/sources-of-power/) (OpenAlex holds only a low-quality record for this title, [W1606313724](https://openalex.org/W1606313724); the MIT Press page is the reliable reference). **ESTABLISHED.**
- **When intuitive expertise can be trusted.** Only in environments of high validity (stable cue–outcome regularities) with adequate opportunity to learn those regularities from feedback; otherwise confident expert intuition is unreliable. [Kahneman & Klein 2009, "Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree", Am Psychologist 64(6):515](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755) (2,456 citations). **ESTABLISHED.**
- **Situation awareness.** Three levels (perception of elements, comprehension, projection); GDTA derives SA requirements from a goal-decision hierarchy. [Endsley 1995](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779049543); [Endsley, Bolte & Jones 2003](https://openalex.org/W1544152542).
- **Work-as-imagined vs work-as-done.** WAI "reflects the way in which we think that work is or should be performed… often embodied in guidelines and protocols"; WAD is "the way in which these working activities take form in actual practice". Standardised procedures "are not always adhered to in practice", and a structural WAI–WAD gap can itself harm safety. [Hollnagel 2017, "Why is Work-as-Imagined Different from Work-as-Done?", in *Resilient Health Care, Volume 2*](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315605739-24); [Hollnagel, *Safety-I and Safety-II*](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315607511); practitioner synthesis: [Pennsylvania Patient Safety Advisory, "Bridging the Gap between Work-as-Imagined and Work-as-Done" (2017)](https://patientsafety.pa.gov/ADVISORIES/pages/201706_80.aspx). Empirical instances: ICU handovers, where "notable discrepancies persist between formal protocols and real-world practices" and clinicians adapt SBAR/I-PASS under interruption and time pressure ([Springer, "From Protocol to Practice: Examining ICU Shift Handovers through Work-as-Done"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-22937-3_14)); community-pharmacy dispensing ([Applied Ergonomics 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003687021000193)). **ESTABLISHED as a phenomenon; sizes are case-specific.**
- **Situated action and situated learning.** Plans are resources for action rather than determinants of it ([Suchman 2007, *Human–Machine Reconfigurations*, the second edition of *Plans and Situated Actions*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511808418)); learning is legitimate peripheral participation in a community of practice ([Lave & Wenger 1991, *Situated Learning*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355), 33,110 citations). **ESTABLISHED as theory; support is ethnographic, not experimental.**

### Inferences
- The WAI/WAD gap tells us what World B artefacts actually are: an *imagined* account of expert practice. In physics teaching specifically, a textbook worked solution is work-as-imagined; the expert's real solution path — false starts, representation switches, sanity checks, abandoned approaches — is work-as-done, and it is visible only in traces or observation. A reconstruct-first pipeline that treats worked solutions as ground truth will inherit exactly the omissions that CTA exists to fix.

### Gaps
- No quantified estimate of the WAI–WAD gap for *cognitive* work (as opposed to procedural safety-critical work) was found.

## 7. Expert–novice differences: which show up in written artefacts, which only in behaviour

### Takeaway
Some expert–novice differences are *representational* and leave textual traces: principle-based categorisation, knowledge organisation, choice of representation in written solutions. Others are *perceptual and fluency* differences: chunk-based perception, pattern-recognition speed, rapid structure extraction. The second class appears only in timed behaviour or performance under constraint, and is acquired through practice rather than by being told.

### Cited Findings
- **Physics categorisation.** Experts sort problems by underlying principle (e.g., conservation of energy), novices by surface features (e.g., inclined plane, spring). **ESTABLISHED**, heavily replicated (5,266 citations). [Chi, Feltovich & Glaser 1981, Cognitive Science 5(2):121](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2) (sample details not re-read).
- **Chunking.** Chess masters reconstruct meaningful positions from brief exposure far better than weaker players, but not random ones; expert perception is chunk-based. **ESTABLISHED** (4,395 citations). [Chase & Simon 1973, Cognitive Psychology 4(1):55](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90004-2).
- **Deliberate practice.** Expert performance reflects extended, effortful, feedback-rich practice. **ESTABLISHED as a major driver; contested on magnitude.** [Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993, Psych Rev 100(3):363](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363) (9,053 citations).
- **Perceptual learning is a neglected component of expertise.** "Learning in educational settings emphasizes declarative and procedural knowledge. Studies of expertise, however, point to other crucial components of learning, especially improvements produced by experience in the extraction of information." Three perceptual-learning modules in middle- and high-school mathematics: matching function information across multiple representations improved generating correct graphs and equations from word problems; practice in *seeing* equation structure across transformations (without solving) "led to dramatic improvements in the speed of equation solving"; a linear-measurement module transferred to novel measurement and fraction problems. **PROMISING→ESTABLISHED**. [Kellman, Massey & Son 2010, Topics in Cognitive Science 2(2):285](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2009.01053.x); review: [Kellman & Garrigan 2009, Physics of Life Reviews 6(2):53](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2008.12.001).
- **Adaptive vs routine expertise.** Routine experts are fast and accurate on familiar tasks; adaptive experts understand why procedures work and can modify or invent them. [Hatano & Inagaki 1986, in Stevenson, Azuma & Hakuta (eds.), *Child Development and Education in Japan*, Freeman](https://openalex.org/W1660388999) — the chapter has no DOI and OpenAlex matches only the edited volume. **ESTABLISHED as a distinction.**

### Inferences
- **Text-visible (World A/B):** principle-based problem classification; conceptual organisation, recoverable as a concept map; canonical representations and heuristics experts *do* write ("draw the free-body diagram first", "check limiting cases"); adaptive-expertise rationales of the form "this works because…".
- **Behaviour-only (World C):** chunk size and perceptual fluency; the order and timing of attention; *which* cue in a particular problem triggered the principle choice; automated error-monitoring and self-check habits. These are the things think-aloud and eye-tracking reveal directly and that CDM reconstructs only partially. For this project, that means the residual after reconstruction is not "more principles" but *cue→principle bindings and checking behaviour*.
- Kellman's results matter strategically: the perceptual component of physics expertise can be *trained* by items rather than by explanation, so a reconstruct-first pipeline that cannot recover perceptual knowledge may still be able to build instruction that produces it, provided the item structure is right.

### Gaps
- No study measures what share of the Chi-style expert–novice differences in physics is already explicit in textbooks or expert-written solutions. That measurement is the direct empirical form of the World A question and does not require any expert time.

## 8. Evidence against the claim that public text can substitute for experts

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is from the sociology of scientific knowledge: skills whose written descriptions were publicly available still failed to transfer without personal contact with a group that already had them. This is a real constraint, but note its exact shape. The failures concern *acquiring a capability to perform*, not *obtaining a description of the practice*. For an instructional-design pipeline, which needs descriptions rather than capability, the analogy is suggestive rather than decisive. In addition, the "externalisation" step assumed by reconstruct-first is contested in its own home literature (§5, Gourlay; Tsoukas), and CTA's own authorities state that observation alone cannot identify mental operations (§3, Clark et al.).

### Cited Findings
- **The TEA laser.** Collins's classic study of laser-building found that laboratories succeeded only after personal contact with an already-successful group, despite published accounts; his methodological point was that research methods based on publications and citation networks cannot reveal "cognitive specificity and discontinuity". **ESTABLISHED** as a foundational qualitative case (627 citations). [Collins 1974, "The TEA Set: Tacit Knowledge and Scientific Networks", Science Studies 4(2):165](https://doi.org/10.1177/030631277400400203). Caveat: the OpenAlex abstract I retrieved states the methodological argument; the specific transfer-failure narrative is from the body of the paper, which I did not re-read.
- **The Q of sapphire.** A high-Q measurement was not reproduced elsewhere for years; replication required direct contact and trust relations with the originating group. **ESTABLISHED** as a qualitative case (445 citations). [Collins 2001, "Tacit Knowledge, Trust and the Q of Sapphire", Social Studies of Science 31(1):71](https://doi.org/10.1177/030631201031001004) (content not re-read).
- **Knowledge embodied in people, not documents.** Tacit knowledge is "embodied in people rather than words, equations, or diagrams", and where a practising community lapses, re-creating the capability "would thus have some of the characteristics of reinvention rather than simply copying" — i.e., the documentary record alone does not restore a practice. **ESTABLISHED** (historical/qualitative). [MacKenzie & Spinardi 1995, American Journal of Sociology 101(1):44](https://doi.org/10.1086/230699).
- **Situated cognition.** Written plans do not determine action; competence is constituted in the situation ([Suchman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511808418)) and acquired through participation ([Lave & Wenger 1991](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355)). **ESTABLISHED as theory.**
- **Somatic and collective tacit knowledge are the hard cases by construction.** Collins's own claim is that STK is "less" explicable and CTK "impossible" (§5). Any reconstruct-first design that claims to recover these is claiming something Collins denies in principle, and should say so explicitly.
- **Observation has limits too.** "Although definitive identification of an expert's mental operations cannot be accomplished through observation, analysts may note the occasions on which it seems that experts must make decisions, assess situations, or engage in analysis." [Clark et al. 2008, p. 579](https://hpttreasures.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cta_chapter_2007-1.pdf). This cuts against a naive World C position as well: watching humans does not by itself yield the cognitive model either.
- **The one quantified "text is missing things" datum** remains Crandall & Getchell-Reiter: 25 of 70 expert cues absent from the contemporaneous training literature (§1). **PROMISING**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/00012272-199309000-00006).

### Inferences
- The honest reading of the sociology-of-science evidence is narrower than it is usually made to sound. It establishes that **published text did not transfer capability** in cases involving fine motor/perceptual craft and unstated local conditions. It does not establish that published text cannot yield an adequate *description* of the cognitive structure of a well-codified academic domain such as introductory mechanics, where the practice is unusually heavily documented, taught and written about. The right expectation is a domain-dependent answer, with physics near the favourable end.
- The genuinely unanswered empirical question, and the one a reconstruct-first project should design for, is: **for a given task, what fraction of a CTA-derived operation list can be recovered from public text alone, and is the residual concentrated in cue/perceptual knowledge as theory predicts?** Nothing I found answers this for any domain.

### Gaps
- I did not find a study that takes an existing CTA output and asks how much of it was recoverable from the literature available at the time. The NICU study is the closest thing (one third of cues missing), and it is from 1993, pre-dating modern digital corpora.
- I deliberately excluded one recent line of evidence from this note: randomised and benchmark work on whether AI assistance transfers to physical laboratory performance in a dual-use biological context. It is relevant methodologically (a text-based assistant produced no significant gain in physical task completion, which is direct evidence that text help does not confer procedural skill), but the material is sensitive and I have left it out rather than summarise it here. If the report writer wants that evidence line, it should be sourced separately and deliberately.

## 9. LLMs performing CTA or knowledge elicitation (2023–2026)

### Takeaway
There is now a small but real literature. The best-evidenced result is **PROMISING**: an LLM given expert *behavioural traces* produced knowledge that supported novice performance better than knowledge experts articulated themselves. The counter-evidence is equally important: in ontology construction, AI-led interviews were faster and more structured but the resulting artefacts "missed a lot of data", and human-generated concept maps still beat LLM maps on contribution to understanding. Nothing yet tests LLM reconstruction against a CTA gold standard.

### Cited Findings
- **LLM externalisation beat expert articulation (the key study).** Two studies in math tutoring. The LLM was given behavioural traces — student–tutor conversations ending in a student mistake, plus the expert teacher's response — and in the strongest condition accessed 320 of 420 training conversations. Study 1 evaluated on 122 complete testing conversations: LLM+Conversation and LLM+Both significantly outperformed the "Human Best Effort" expert-articulated baseline (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01). Study 2 trained human novices on the externalised knowledge (final n per arm: LLM Baseline 85, LLM+Both 94, LLM+Conversation 91, Human Best Effort 87, No Tacit Knowledge 99; each responding to five conversations). The expert–novice gap was significantly smaller under LLM+Conversation than Human Best Effort (p < 0.05), and novices matched expert "caring" ratings (p = 0.796). Authors' stated limits: a single context (math tutoring) and short-term learning effects only. **PROMISING** (preprint, one domain). [Cho, Funk, Gupta & Yang 2026, arXiv:2608.07488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07488). The expert-articulated baseline comes from [Wang, Zhang, Robinson, Loeb & Demszky 2024, NAACL](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.naacl-long.120).
- **AI-led interviews: faster, more standardised, less complete.** ChatGPT-4 conducted knowledge-elicitation interviews in parallel with human-expert interviews; outputs were turned into RDF ontologies through several pipelines and compared against a base-truth ontology using OQuaRE metrics and structural analysis. "AI-led interviews are more time-efficient and structured compared to human expert interviews. However, a human approach works better for ontology generation: AI-generated ontologies are more standardized but missed a lot of data, whereas human-created ontologies captured more…". **PROMISING** (single study, under review). [van den Bent, Pernisch & Schlobach, "Investigating Knowledge Elicitation Automation with Large Language Models", Semantic Web Journal submission](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/content/investigating-knowledge-elicitation-automation-large-language-models); [PDF](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj3868.pdf).
- **LLM concept maps vs expert concept maps.** In a Qatar University anatomy study, 74 medical students rated three concept maps (LLM-generated, LAM-generated, human-expert) on clarity, structure and scientific accuracy. "Human-generated maps demonstrated the highest ratings for contribution to understanding, while clarity ratings were comparable between the human-generated and LLM maps"; students preferred the human map across categories. **PROMISING** (n = 74 raters, one topic). [Medical Principles and Practice 2025](https://karger.com/mpp/article/doi/10.1159/000552430/948833/Blending-Artificial-Intelligence-with-Expertise-A); [PMC copy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13341128/).
- **Systematic review of LLM concept-map generation.** Validation strategies range from precision/recall/F1 and semantic similarity to expert review and learner feedback; the review reports promise for "scalable, adaptive, and pedagogically relevant knowledge visualization" while flagging open problems of validity, interpretability and classroom integration. **PROMISING as a map of the field**. [Zhai et al. 2025, arXiv:2509.14554](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14554).
- **Automating knowledge-component identification.** "Knowledge component identification and extraction have traditionally depended on human effort through cognitive task analysis, a manual process that requires domain experts and is highly labor-intensive"; the paper applies LLMs to automatic KC extraction. Directly relevant to the diagnosis half of this project. [Wang et al. 2025, arXiv:2511.09935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09935) (abstract body not fully retrieved; results not verified).
- **LLM-mediated elicitation from SMEs as a tool pattern.** "Data Therapist" uses an LLM to elicit domain knowledge from subject-matter experts through mixed-initiative questioning and to structure the result. [Rahman et al. 2025, arXiv:2505.00455](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00455) (abstract not fully retrieved). Related: expert-knowledge-to-ontology pipelines ([arXiv:2506.08422](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.08422)), LLMs as priors in place of expert elicitation ([Selby et al. 2025, *Stat*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sta4.70054)), and LLM interviewing competence benchmarks in requirements elicitation ([ReqElicitGym, arXiv:2602.18306](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18306); [follow-up question generation, arXiv:2507.02858](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02858)).

### Inferences
- The Cho et al. design is the single most important precedent for this project, and its crucial feature is easy to miss: the LLM did **not** work from public text. It worked from **expert behavioural traces** (conversations plus expert responses). It therefore supports "reconstruct from *traces* first" much more strongly than "reconstruct from *public text* first". Traces are World B, not World A.
- Taken together, the LLM results have a consistent shape: LLMs are strong at *structuring and standardising* knowledge and at *inferring policy from observed expert decisions*, and weak at *completeness* against a human-built reference. That is the same access profile the differential-access literature would predict for a technique built on conceptual regularities rather than on episodes.
- Consequence for design: the highest-value cheap experiment is not "can an LLM describe physics expertise" (it can produce something plausible) but "how does LLM-reconstructed operation list compare, item by item, against a CTA-derived list, and where are the misses". That is a coding study against an existing gold standard and needs no new expert sessions.

### Gaps
- No study I found applies an LLM to a *CTA* gold standard (CDM/PARI/ACTA output) and reports recall/precision against it. This is the direct test of the reconstruct-first premise and appears not to exist yet.
- No study compares LLM reconstruction from public text alone against LLM reconstruction from traces. Cho et al. include an "LLM Baseline" arm without conversations, which is the closest available comparison, and it performed worse — but the numbers for that arm were not retrieved beyond the significance tests reported above.

## Implications for reconstruct-first strategy

A mapping of knowledge types to the three worlds. Tags are the confidence that the *placement* is right, not that the underlying research is sound.

### World A — recoverable from public text (an LLM with the literature)
- **Principles, concepts, canonical solution schemas, taxonomy of problem types.** Physics is unusually well codified; this is what textbooks are. **ESTABLISHED** placement.
- **Principle-based problem categorisation** as a *scheme* (energy vs momentum vs kinematics). The expert–novice difference itself is documented (Chi et al.), and the scheme is publishable text. **ESTABLISHED.**
- **Explicitly taught heuristics and representational conventions** ("draw the free-body diagram", "check limiting cases", "count unknowns vs equations"). **ESTABLISHED.**
- **Already-explicated relational tacit knowledge** — whatever some expert somewhere has written down. Chao & Salvendy's aggregation result implies that pooling across many written sources recovers much more than any single expert would say. **PROMISING.**
- **Adaptive-expertise rationales** ("why this method works here"), which are the staple of good textbooks and physics-education papers. **PROMISING.**
- Caution: what is in public text is largely **work-as-imagined** (Hollnagel). Worked solutions are cleaned-up accounts, not traces.

### World B — organisational artefacts (student scripts, traces, course materials, logs)
- **Learner bottlenecks and error distributions.** Not obtainable from experts at all — the expert-blind-spot and curse-of-expertise results say experts systematically mis-rank novice difficulty (Nathan & Petrosino; Hinds). This is the project's K0 territory, and it is correctly placed in scripts rather than in interviews. **ESTABLISHED.**
- **Cue→decision policy inferred from observed expert choices.** This is exactly what Cho et al. did, and it beat expert self-report. Requires traces of experts *acting*, not experts *explaining*. **PROMISING** (one domain, preprint).
- **Which representation an expert actually reaches for, and when they switch.** Visible in written solutions and work products, absent from prose descriptions. **PROMISING.**
- **Work-as-done deviations from the stated method** — recoverable only from artefacts of actual work, and expected to differ from protocols. **ESTABLISHED as a phenomenon.**

### World C — humans only (observation or probed interview required)
- **Cue and expectancy knowledge tied to specific episodes.** The NICU result is the quantified case: one third of the cues experts used were not in the literature. CDM exists because this knowledge is not otherwise obtainable. **PROMISING→ESTABLISHED.**
- **Perceptual fluency and chunking.** Chunk-based perception and rapid structure extraction show up only in timed behaviour (Chase & Simon; Kellman). Text can describe *that* experts see structure; it cannot yield the discrimination. **ESTABLISHED.**
- **Automated procedural micro-steps and "how to do it" detail.** The Sullivan finding that experts articulated "how" for only 13% of action steps is about exactly this layer, and if experts do not say it, it is not in text either. **PROMISING.**
- **Somatic tacit knowledge** (Collins STK) — largely irrelevant for physics problem solving, decisive for laboratory and instrument work.
- **Collective tacit knowledge** (Collins CTK) — disciplinary judgement about what counts as a good argument, what is worth checking, when to stop. Collins holds this is inexplicable in principle. **ESTABLISHED as a theoretical claim; untested empirically.**
- **Self-monitoring and error-checking habits** that are automated and hence unreported.

### Consequences for the project's plan
1. **The reorientation is defensible, but its evidence base is thinner than the CTA rhetoric suggests.** The "70%" figure will not survive scrutiny as a quantitative premise (n = 3–6 studies, circular gold standards, one research group). Argue from Tofel-Grehl & Feldon's g = 0.871 and the surgery SMDs instead: those establish that recovering omitted knowledge is instructionally valuable, which is the premise that actually matters.
2. **The strongest supporting precedent argues for traces, not public text.** Cho et al. gave the model expert behavioural traces. If this project reads it as licence to reconstruct from the literature alone, it is over-claiming. Reconstruct-first should be staged: public text → existing artefacts and traces → human residual.
3. **Aim the human residual at cues and expectancies.** Both the theory (Collins RTK/STK/CTK; Anderson) and the evidence (NICU cues; Chao & Salvendy's 62% ceiling on "interpretations"; differential access) converge on cue→principle bindings, expectancies and checking behaviour as what text cannot supply. That is a much narrower expert protocol than a full CTA, which is exactly what makes the reorientation cheap.
4. **Two cheap studies would de-risk the whole reorientation, and neither needs new expert sessions.** (a) Take a published CTA output (the NICU cue list, or a surgical task list) and measure what fraction an LLM recovers from the literature, reporting recall and where the misses fall. (b) Measure what fraction of the Chi-style expert–novice representational differences in mechanics is explicit in standard textbooks. Neither exists in the literature I searched.
5. **Do not expect reconstruction to fix diagnosis.** The learner half of the problem (which steps are bottlenecks) is a World B question answered by student data. The expert-blind-spot literature is unambiguous that neither experts nor, by extension, text written by experts is a reliable source for it.
6. **Validation is possible without heavy expert time.** The Sternberg inventories show that practical know-how can be probed by written scenarios with expert-rated options. Experts can validate reconstructed knowledge as *raters* in minutes, which is a far lighter ask than a CTA session and could serve as a gate before committing to full expert sessions.
